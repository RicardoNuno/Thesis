# yang2020: Association of problematic smartphone use with poor sleep quality, depression, and anxiety: A systematic review and meta-analysis

**Authors / venue / year:** Yang, Fu, Liao & Li / Psychiatry Research 284, article 112686 / 2020
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psychres.2019.112686
**Relevance:** high

## Summary (3 sentences)

A systematic review and meta-analysis of how problematic smartphone use (PSU) relates to sleep and mental health. People with PSU have higher risk of poor sleep quality, depression and anxiety. Results are also compared between developed and developing countries.

## Method

- Systematic review and meta-analysis of observational studies, PRISMA.
- Databases: EBSCO PsycARTICLES, EMBASE, PubMed, Web of Science, 2010 to June 2019 (from the iPhone 3G onwards, so smartphones, not older phones).
- 2366 records, 14 studies in the review, 13 in the meta-analysis: 13 cross-sectional, 1 cohort.
- Six countries: Korea (5), Turkey (3), China (3), Lebanon, Iran, Spain (1 each). Mostly students.
- PSU measured with validated scales (SAS, SAS-SV, K-SAPS, MPPUS, ...). Outcomes: PSQI (sleep), BDI / CES-D (depression), BAI / SAS (anxiety).
- Odds ratios with 95% CIs; random-effect model when I² > 50%.
- Subgroups: developed vs developing country, adjusted for age, univariate vs multivariate, outcome measure, publication year.

## Results / evidence

- Poor sleep quality: OR 2.19 (95% CI 1.79–2.67), 8 studies, I² = 56.9%.
- Depression: OR 2.88 (95% CI 2.30–3.79), 6 studies, I² = 74.2%.
- Anxiety: OR 3.50 (95% CI 2.20–5.57), 5 studies, I² = 86.2%.
- No single study changed the overall results (sensitivity analysis). No significant publication bias (Egger's test).
- Developed vs developing countries (Table 2):

  | Outcome | Developed | Developing |
  | ------- | --------- | ---------- |
  | Poor sleep | 1.95 (3 studies) | 2.42 (5 studies) |
  | Depression | 2.93 (2 studies) | 2.62 (4 studies) |
  | Anxiety | 4.41 (1 study) | 3.12 (4 studies) |

  PSU is linked to worse sleep in developing countries, but to more depression and anxiety in developed ones. The developed subgroups are tiny (1–3 studies), so treat this as weak. The authors note that people in developed economies are more likely to own smartphones.
- Publication year: recent studies (2018–2019) show higher ORs for depression (4.27) and anxiety (4.50). The authors read this as the link getting stronger as mobile technology develops.

## Strengths / limitations

- Strength: first meta-analysis to give effect sizes for PSU with sleep, depression and anxiety together. Two independent reviewers, quality rated with the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (5–8 out of 9).
- Limitation: only 14 studies, moderate to high heterogeneity.
- Limitation: almost all cross-sectional, so association, not causation. Poor sleep, depression and anxiety may also increase PSU (the authors say so).
- Limitation: self-reported PSU; mostly students; more women than men; only six countries, mostly in Asia.
- Limitation: many studies use univariate analysis without controlling for age or other confounders. Adjusted estimates are lower (e.g. sleep 1.95 adjusted for age vs 2.42 unadjusted).

## How it relates to my thesis

Same direction as sohn2019, with similar sizes (roughly 2–3.5 times the odds), and not limited to children: PSU goes together with poor sleep, depression and anxiety, so reducing it is worth doing. The authors call PSU "a global public health problem" and say people should "reduce and avoid its adverse effects" (p. 8).

Also useful against panova2018:
- The introduction cites studies linking PSU to musculoskeletal pain (Xie et al. 2016) and traffic and pedestrian accidents (Cazzulino et al. 2014) (p. 1).
- It cites Rozgonjuk et al. (2018): anxiety was related to the number of screen unlocks, measured objectively (p. 7). That is a device-level behaviour, not an app.
- The authors themselves suggest that apps logging phone use can replace self-report (p. 7).
