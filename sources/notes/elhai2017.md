# elhai2017: Problematic smartphone use: A conceptual overview and systematic review of relations with anxiety and depression psychopathology

**Authors / venue / year:** Elhai, Dvorak, Levine & Hall / Journal of Affective Disorders 207, 251–259 / 2017
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jad.2016.08.030
**Relevance:** high

## Summary (3 sentences)

A conceptual overview of problematic smartphone use (PSU) plus a systematic review of how it relates to depression, anxiety, stress and self-esteem. Depression is consistently linked to PSU with medium effects, anxiety and stress with small effects, and self-esteem only inconsistently. Its main value is the explanation of *why*: how PSU develops (reinforcement and five pathways) and how it connects to mental health (three causal directions).

## Method

- Systematic review (PRISMA), no meta-analysis: effect sizes (r, beta) reported and compared, not pooled.
- PsycINFO, PubMed, Google Scholar, 2008 to September 2015 (post-iPhone). 117 titles, 23 studies included.
- Mostly students (college 10, school 6), samples of 200+ (mean 623). Mostly China, Korea, US (4 each).
- Outcomes: only constructs studied in at least three papers, so depression, anxiety, stress, self-esteem. Sleep is not a reviewed outcome.
- Only 6 of 23 studies clearly sampled smartphone (not older phone) users, so studies since 2014 are also reported separately.

## Results / evidence

- **Depression:** significant in 9 of 10 studies; r ≈ .30–.40 (medium).
- **Anxiety:** significant in 8 of 9; r ≈ .20 (small).
- **Stress:** significant in 5 of 6; r ≈ .20–.30 (small to medium).
- **Self-esteem:** lower self-esteem with more PSU in 5 of 6 bivariate, r = −.10 to −.30, but mostly near zero after adjusting for other variables (inconsistent).
- Adjusting for other variables gives similar but smaller effects. Studies since 2014 give the same picture.

### How PSU develops (Section 3, pp. 252–253)

- **Positive reinforcement** (incentive sensitisation): use starts because it feels good (e.g. checking notifications). Through Pavlovian learning, small cues start to signal the reward. Over time "wanting" separates from "liking": people keep wanting to check even when they no longer enjoy it.
- **Negative reinforcement** ("self-medication"): use relieves negative emotion. Once compulsive, not using causes negative mood (withdrawal), and only using relieves it.
- Five pathways (from Billieux et al. 2015), not mutually exclusive:
  1. **Habit and checking:** notifications and alerts act as cues for automatic checking. Eventually "merely seeing the phone on the countertop or nightstand" triggers checking. Habits are "a gateway" to problematic use (Oulasvirta et al. 2012).
  2. **Excessive reassurance seeking:** checking for reassurance from friends and partners; driven by low self-esteem, loneliness, depression, anxiety.
  3. **Fear of missing out (FoMO):** need to stay connected to what others are doing.
  4. **Extraversion:** drive to keep and build relationships; sensation and reward seeking.
  5. **Impulsivity:** lack of self-control over use.

### How PSU and mental health connect (Section 7.1, p. 257)

1. **Mental health problems cause PSU:** depressed or anxious people use the phone to escape negative emotion (experiential avoidance), which does not work and makes things worse. Fits the reassurance-seeking pathway.
2. **PSU causes mental health problems:** heavy use predicts later stress, sleep problems and depression (Thomée et al. 2011, one-year follow-up, even excluding people with problems at baseline). Mechanisms: night use keeps people awake; blue light disrupts sleep; work pressure to stay connected causes stress and burnout.
3. **Both (vicious cycle):** a depressed person uses the phone to escape, stays up late, gets more depressed and stressed, uses more.

## Strengths / limitations

- Strength: reports effect sizes, not just significance; separates bivariate from adjusted results.
- Strength: clear theoretical framework for causes, used by many later papers.
- Limitation: only correlational studies; no pooled estimate (that is what yang2020 adds).
- Limitation: many studies may include non-smartphone users; mostly students; mostly Asia and US.
- Limitation: many different PSU scales, some measure only amount of use (calls, texts), not problematic use.
- Limitation: the causal pathways come from theory and earlier reviews, not from the 23 studies themselves.

## How it relates to my thesis

The most useful paper so far for the *mechanism*. The habit pathway says the cues are the notification and the sight of the phone itself. That is the device-level argument against panova2018: checking is triggered by the device, not by any one app. A smartwatch can remove the phone as a visible cue and filter which notifications reach the user. But the watch also delivers notifications, so it could just move the cue to the wrist. The thesis needs to address that.

The "wanting vs liking" split also explains why people keep checking a phone they don't enjoy, which matches the "need vs habit" distinction: if the watch covers the needs, any remaining urge to check is habit.

The vicious cycle (point 3 above) supports intervening on use itself, not only on mental health.
