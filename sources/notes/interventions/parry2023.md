# parry2023: Digital wellbeing applications: Adoption, use and perceived effects

**Authors / venue / year:** Parry, le Roux, Morton, Pons, Pretorius & Schoeman / Computers in Human Behavior 139, 107542 / 2023 (online 2022)
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2022.107542 (preprint: https://osf.io/6e9ap)
**Relevance:** medium

_Skimmed from the OSF preprint (May 2022), so wording and numbers may differ slightly from the published version; quotes cite sections._

## Summary (3 sentences)

Pre-registered mixed-methods study of 1,849 South African undergraduates (survey, mid-2020) plus 20 interviews with users, on who uses digital wellbeing apps, why, and with what perceived effect. 80% knew such apps but only 37% used them (9% regularly), mostly iOS Screen Time's weekly reports; iPhone owners used them far more than Android owners (50% vs 28%), and users and non-users reported the same daily phone time. Most users were "passive and occasional" (look at reports, change nothing); a small "active and regular" group used limits and blocks, mostly around exams, and interviewees stressed that the app only helps if the person is already motivated.

## Method

- Survey emailed to all undergraduates at Stellenbosch University (mid-2020, during COVID); 1,849 valid (63% female, mean age 20.5; 58% Android, 42% iPhone).
- Measures (developed for the study): awareness and use (never / ceased / occasional / regular), apps and features used, self-estimated daily phone minutes, 7-point items for integration into daily life and four motivations (information seeking, negative outcomes, behaviour alignment with goals, behaviour change), and perceived usefulness.
- Pre-registered hypotheses that all four motivations predict integration; multiple regression on 683 users.
- 20 interviews (~40 min, online) randomly drawn from 322 volunteers; inductive thematic analysis.

## Results / evidence

- Awareness 80%; use 37% (27.8% occasional, 9.1% regular); 16% stopped; 47% never used.
- Apps: iOS Screen Time 52%, Forest 18%, Huawei Digital Balance 14%, Android Digital Wellbeing 13%, AppBlock 6%. Features: weekly overall report 66%, disabling notifications 44%, weekly category report 44%, daily usage 41%, app timers 36%, blocking specific apps 18%.
- iPhone owners 50% users vs Android 28% (Screen Time pushes weekly reports by default); women 40% vs men 32%; users of mental-health and fitness apps more likely to use them.
- Integration predicted by information seeking (B = .28), behaviour alignment (B = .28) and behaviour change (B = .23); negative experiences with the phone predicted it *negatively* (B = −.10). R² = .37.
- Self-reported phone time: users 330 vs non-users 338 min/day (no difference).
- Perceived effects: 68% found the data interesting/useful, but only 52% felt it helped change behaviour and 51% that it helped them stay on task.
- Two user styles: **active and regular** (check stats often, set limits, e.g. "45 minutes a day on Instagram", downtime after 20:00) vs **passive and occasional** ("it just tells me at the end of the week… and then I just keep on doing what I was doing before").
- Native tools seen as easy to bypass; third-party apps used for restriction but put off by ads and unwillingness to pay. Many prefer non-technical strategies (phone in another room, airplane mode, deleting apps, offline hobbies), especially during exams.
- "It's not the apps that are going to do it for me" (P20): success depends on prior motivation and commitment.

## Strengths / limitations

- Strength: large sample, pre-registered, open materials and data; combines prevalence numbers with interviews about lived use.
- Strength: distinguishes active vs passive users and native vs third-party tools.
- Limitation: phone time self-estimated with a slider, the very measure parry2021 (same first author) shows to be unreliable; the "no difference" finding could reflect estimation error.
- Limitation: binary use measure; new, partly validated scales; cross-sectional, so no causal effect of the apps.
- Limitation: one South African university, during COVID-19.
- Limitation: the discussion says H1, H2 and H4 were supported, but the results support H1, H3 and H4 (H2 went the opposite way).

## How it relates to my thesis

Context for the baseline comparison with Digital Wellbeing in my proposal: most people who "use" these tools only glance at weekly reports, Android's built-in tool is used far less than iOS Screen Time, and passive reports rarely change behaviour, so a prototype should do something at the moment of use rather than only report afterwards (cf. hiniker2016, kim2019lockntype, haliburton2024). It also explains why third-party apps struggle: users want restriction but find native tools easy to bypass and won't pay for apps with ads (cf. why many wellbeing apps disappear). Its point that results depend on motivation echoes mark2018 and hiniker2016 (reduction-focused users benefit most), which argues for measuring participants' motivation as a moderator in an evaluation. Methodologically, its self-reported phone time is a reminder to log usage instead (parry2021).
