# ko2015: NUGU: A Group-based Intervention App for Improving Self-Regulation of Limiting Smartphone Use

**Authors / venue / year:** Ko, Yang, Lee, Heizmann, Jeong, Lee, Shin, Yatani, Song & Chung / CSCW '15, 1235–1245 / 2015
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1145/2675133.2675244
**Relevance:** medium

## Summary (3 sentences)

A survey (n = 114) found people try to limit phone use (putting it away, muting, deleting apps) but the strategies fail because they rely on willpower; NUGU ("No Use is Good Use") is an Android app built on social cognitive theory where users start timed "limiting missions" (10 min–2 h, tagged with an activity) that lock the phone, earn points, and are shared and ranked within a group of friends. In a 3-week study (1 baseline week, 2 intervention weeks), friend groups using NUGU (n = 35) cut daily use from 234 to 177 min and app launches from 234 to 181, while people using a version without the social features (n = 27) did not change significantly. Social comparison (rankings, seeing others' limiting) was the strongest motivator, and group users set more, longer and more varied missions.

## Method

- Survey (n = 114, mostly students): 64% felt they overused, 60% wanted to change. Two problem types: frequent short use (notifications, checking) and occasional long use (browsing, games, at night). Strategies: physical separation 31.5%, turning off/muting 27%, deleting apps 17.5%, limiting apps 9.6%; they "often failed and did not last long".
- Review of 41 Play Store intervention apps: 71% used locking, 68% self-monitoring; only one used social support.
- NUGU features: (1) self-monitoring (hourly usage vs limiting, app icons); (2) limiting mission: choose duration and activity, phone locked except calls, silent mode, a goal screen with remaining time and a give-up button with "Do you really want to give up?"; points proportional to time plus a bonus for completing; (3) individual ranking within the group and group ranking between groups. Two one-week pilots (n = 10, 20) refined it (e.g. global ranking replaced by group ranking because strangers didn't matter).
- Evaluation: quasi-experiment, Korean university; NUGU-Group 35 people in 8 friend groups (3–6, incl. a family), NUGU-Alone 27 (same app without rankings); assignment not random. Logged usage; SAS (33 items) and adapted self-efficacy scale pre/post; exit interviews; $50–60.

## Results / evidence

- Usage, group: time 234 → 177 min/day (p < .001, d = 0.99), app launches 234 → 181 (d = 1.14). Alone: 243 → 232 min and 225 → 228 launches (n.s.). Post-intervention, group used significantly less than alone.
- SAS fell in both; more in the group, mainly on daily-life disturbance.
- Missions: group users set 5.6 per day vs 1.3 alone; 66% over 30 min vs 47%; more varied activities (entropy 0.70 vs 0.37), copying top rankers. Success rate ~77% in both; almost half of give-ups happened in the first 10 minutes.
- Motivation: rankings and catching up with friends; small group rituals (snacks for the top ranker, "king of limiting"); closer groups (family, close friends) did better than a weekly church acquaintance group.
- Self-monitoring helped people notice the problem but not act on it; users forgot to start missions and asked for reminders or automatic suggestions. They wanted some apps (dictionary, notes) allowed during missions.

## Strengths / limitations

- Strength: logged usage; compares the same app with and without social features; rich account of how social comparison works.
- Strength: design details grounded in pilots (mission durations, give-up confirmation, points).
- Limitation: **not randomised**: group condition was friends who signed up together, so motivation and cohesion differ from the solo condition.
- Limitation: 2 weeks of intervention, 62 students, no follow-up.
- Limitation: "No use is good use" framing: success measured as less use overall, not unwanted use.
- Limitation: text says self-efficacy "decreased" in both groups, but the table shows it increased (26.5 → 29.4 and 26.6 → 28.1).

## How it relates to my thesis

The first study comparing a social vs non-social version of the same phone-limiting app: the social version reduced logged use and launches, the solo one didn't, so self-monitoring plus voluntary locking alone was not enough here. Same team and idea as ko2016 (Lock n' LoL), which made it synchronous and co-located. Relevant if social features are considered; otherwise mainly useful for its design lessons: a confirm dialog before giving up, a "most failures in the first 10 minutes" pattern, users forgetting to start sessions (→ reminders or automatic triggers, cf. kim2017pomodolock), and the need to whitelist useful apps. Its survey also repeats that self-chosen strategies fail because they rely on willpower (cf. lyngs2019's dual-systems explanation).
