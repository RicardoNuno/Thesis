# vanvelthoven2018: Problematic smartphone use: Digital approaches to an emerging public health problem

**Authors / venue / year:** van Velthoven, Powell & Powell / Digital Health 4, 2055207618759167 / 2018
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1177/2055207618759167 (open access; PMC6034350)
**Relevance:** low

## Summary (3 sentences)

Short commentary framing problematic smartphone use as a public health problem, summarising why it happens (constant access, intermittent variable rewards, fear of missing out, personality routes from Billieux) and what has been tried (digital detoxes, tips, apps). The authors searched alternativeto.net (Dec 2016, updated Oct 2017) and tabulate 21 mobile apps for regulating phone use, most of which track use and block apps or functions. They conclude that no approach has evidence yet, and list four challenges for apps: no efficacy evidence, no theoretical basis or tailoring, data protection concerns, and technical issues such as battery drain.

## Method

- Narrative commentary, no systematic review method for the background.
- App overview: one search of alternativeto.net; 48 apps found, 27 desktop-only excluded, 21 mobile apps kept, plus SPACE (launched Oct 2017). Table 1 lists aim, features, platform and price as described by each app's website; no app was installed or tested.

## Results / evidence

- Most apps monitor use and block functions for a period or at set times; only Forest uses a game mechanic (growing a tree).
- Many listed apps are Android-only (AppDetox, Cold Turkey, Stay Focused, Self Control for Study…), several are parental control, and some (ClockIn Portal, Time Doctor, Agendrix) are employee time-tracking tools rather than wellbeing apps.
- Challenges for apps:
  1. no evidence of safety or efficacy, and measures such as PUMP and MPPUS are not widely validated;
  2. most lack a psychological basis, are not tailored to different user types, and block rather than address motivation; people become desensitised to repeated messages;
  3. data security and third-party sharing;
  4. technical and design issues, e.g. "an app which drains battery power may not be used for this reason".
- Points out that logging is more accurate than self-report because people underestimate use and checking frequency.
- Digital detox: of the UK adults who had tried one, a quarter stayed offline for a day and 5% for a month (Ofcom figures); compared to crash diets.

## Strengths / limitations

- Strength: early, compact statement of why app-based interventions lacked evidence in 2018, with a concrete list of challenges.
- Limitation: not externally peer-reviewed ("Editorial content: internally reviewed").
- Limitation: conflict of interest: the third author founded SPACE, which is in the app table, and the first author advises its company.
- Limitation: the app list comes from one website, is not systematic, mixes in employee-tracking and parental apps, and was not tested.
- Limitation: the claim that no app has been assessed was already outdated by 2018 (e.g. ko2015, hiniker2016, okeke2018, lyngs2019's review).
- Limitation: much of the harm evidence comes from a UK Ofcom survey and press-style sources.

## How it relates to my thesis

Mostly useful for its list of challenges, three of which match my proposal's evaluation criteria: efficacy (accuracy and effect against Digital Wellbeing), acceptability and data protection (consent-friendly design), and battery drain as a reason apps get abandoned. The desensitisation point supports the habituation theme from haliburton2024 and almourad2021. The app table and its prevalence figures are weak and dated, so it should not be cited as evidence of app effectiveness or as a market review.
