# kasturiratna2025: A multifaceted nudge-based intervention to reduce smartphone use: Findings from a randomized cross-over trial

**Authors / venue / year:** Kasturiratna, Chua & Hartanto / Mobile Media & Communication 13(3), 504–525 / 2025
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1177/20501579251323246 (author copy: https://ink.library.smu.edu.sg/soss_research/4186; data and code: https://researchbox.org/3502)
**Relevance:** medium

_Skimmed from the SMU repository copy (original manuscript); page numbers are the manuscript's 1–22, not the journal's 504–525._

## Summary (3 sentences)

163 Singaporean undergraduates spent five days with a bundle of phone-setting nudges (silent mode, grayscale, no face/touch ID with a 16-digit passcode, social apps removed from the home screen) and five days with normal settings, in counterbalanced order with a washout in between. With the nudges, daily screen time dropped by about 26 minutes and pickups by about 3 a day (both statistically significant but small), while productivity, sleep and cognitive failures didn't change and stress went up. The authors conclude that cheap setting changes can reduce use, but making the phone harder and duller to use without fully removing it can frustrate people, and the bundle doesn't show which nudge did what.

## Method

- Randomised within-subject cross-over: baseline survey, then 5 daily diaries per condition, 41–48 h washout, conditions swapped; order counterbalanced. Not pre-registered; data and code open.
- Experimental settings: silent ringer, grayscale display, face/touch ID off with 16-digit passcode, social apps off the home screen. Control: vibrate, colour, biometrics with 4-digit passcode, normal home screen.
- Usage: daily screenshots of iOS Screen Time / Android Digital Wellbeing (screen time, pickups/unlocks), hand-coded by the authors.
- Daily self-reports: productivity (3 items), sleep quality (1 item), stress (4-item PSS), cognitive failures (13 items); a daily honesty check on settings changes.
- Multilevel models (linear for time, Poisson for pickups) with condition, order and their interaction; S$19 or course credit.

## Results / evidence

- Screen time: control ≈ 6.5 h/day vs nudges ≈ 6.05 h (−0.44 h, β = −.08, p < .001).
- Pickups: ≈ 138 vs 135 per day (−3, β = −.03, p = .001).
- Stress higher with nudges (β = .05, p = .035), but only when the nudge week came second (after a normal week); no difference when it came first.
- No effect on productivity, sleep quality or cognitive failures.
- No moderation by sex or household income.
- Authors' own reading: partial restriction (still using the phone, but with friction and less appeal) is frustrating; future work should test nudges separately.

## Strengths / limitations

- Strength: randomised cross-over with counterbalancing and washout; device-reported usage rather than estimates; cheap, realistic intervention anyone can apply.
- Strength: measures both screen time and checking, as two distinct outcomes.
- Limitation: effects are very small (≈ 7% less time, ≈ 2% fewer pickups) although the authors call the time effect "moderate".
- Limitation: only 5 days per condition, so it captures the first days of adjustment, not habituation.
- Limitation: bundle of four nudges, so it can't say which one worked or which one caused the stress.
- Limitation: screenshot-based usage data (daily totals, no timing or context); stress and other outcomes self-reported, with very low within-person reliability for some scales (stress α_within = .17).
- Limitation: undergraduates in Singapore only; not pre-registered.
- Limitation: cites wilcockson2019 as showing lower stress after abstinence, but that study found no change in anxiety or mood.

## How it relates to my thesis

Another data point that light friction without blocking has only small effects: a 16-digit passcode and grayscale barely changed pickups, which is notable next to kim2019lockntype, where a friction task at each app launch halved launches of the target apps. Friction applied to the whole phone (unlock) seems weaker than friction applied to specific apps at the moment of opening them. The type of friction also differs: here it was a fixed 16-digit passcode the participant chose, which quickly becomes muscle memory and can be typed on autopilot, whereas LocknType showed new random digits each time that had to be read and copied (with frequent errors), so it couldn't become automatic. Friction that can't be habituated may be what forces the moment of reflection (my inference from comparing the two; neither paper tests it). It also repeats the trade-off from kim2019goalkeeper and mongeroffarello2019: making the phone more annoying can raise stress. Useful as a baseline of "settings anyone can change" (grayscale, silent, home-screen layout) to compare a prototype against, alongside Digital Wellbeing. Same short-exposure problem as radtke2021.
