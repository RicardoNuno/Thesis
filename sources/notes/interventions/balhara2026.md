# balhara2026: Therapeutic Interventions Targeted at Problematic Use of Digital Technology: Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Evidence

**Authors / venue / year:** Balhara, Bhattacharjee, Bhatia, Sanahan, Ganesh, Sarkar, Ranjan & Kattimani / JMIR Mental Health 13, e89280 / 2026
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.2196/89280 (open access; PMC13167059)
**Relevance:** medium

_Skimmed from the Europe PMC full text; JMIR articles have no page numbers, so quotes cite the section._

## Summary (3 sentences)

Pre-registered systematic review and meta-analysis of 125 intervention studies (73 RCTs, >8,000 participants, up to April 2025) treating problematic internet, gaming, smartphone, social media, gambling, pornography and screen-time use. Psychological therapies (CBT, mindfulness, ACT and similar) gave the most consistent benefit across conditions; digital and exercise-based programs also helped, while medication, brain stimulation, VR, education and parent programs had limited or early evidence. Heterogeneity and small-study bias were high, most studies were short, self-reported and done in schools or universities, and several behaviours (social media, screen time) had too few studies to pool.

## Method

- PRISMA 2020, PROSPERO-registered; PubMed, Scopus, Embase to April 2025; English, peer-reviewed; RCTs, non-RCTs, pre-post and pilot studies with quantitative outcomes. Excluded prevention-only and offline gambling studies.
- 6,102 records → 1,206 screened → 125 included (κ = 0.98). Countries: China 39, South Korea 15, Iran 11, Germany 9, US 9.
- Targets: internet addiction 52, gaming 35, smartphone 23, gambling 5, screen time 4, social media 3, pornography 3.
- Risk of bias: RoB 2 for RCTs, ROBINS-I for others. Random-effects meta-analyses (SMD, DerSimonian–Laird) by condition × intervention type; any intervention delivered on a digital platform counted as "digital".

## Results / evidence

- Intervention types: psychological 75 studies; digital 22; exercise 7; educational 6; multicomponent 5; pharmacological 4; neuromodulation (tDCS) 3; parent-focused 2; VR 1.
- **Internet addiction:** psychological vs control SMD −2.68 (I² = 96%, small-study bias); digital −1.16 (I² = 87%).
- **Smartphone addiction:** psychological −1.49 (I² = 91%, no clear bias); exercise (Qigong, tai chi, basketball, treadmill) −3.07 (I² = 97%, 3 studies).
- **Gaming disorder:** psychological −1.01 (p = .02), mixed.
- Not poolable: social media, screen time, gambling, pornography; no studies on streaming ("OTT") content.
- Smartphone-specific digital interventions (2 studies): a 20-day app with mindfulness and impulse-control modules reduced use and screen time, but so did the control; a 30-day online mindfulness protocol reduced addiction scores.
- Digital programs worked better with reminders, guidance and steady practice than unguided; education alone gave modest effects unless it added active tasks or self-monitoring.
- RCT risk of bias: 62% some concerns, 27% low, 11% high; non-randomised: 46% serious, 11% critical.
- Authors' own limitations: small samples, short follow-up, mostly self-report rather than device logs, unclear dose and adherence.

## Strengths / limitations

- Strength: broadest synthesis so far across behaviours and intervention types; pre-registered, dual screening, formal risk-of-bias tools.
- Limitation: effect sizes are implausibly large (SMD −2.7 to −3.1) with extreme heterogeneity (I² 87–97%) and small-study bias, so the pooled numbers shouldn't be quoted as real effect magnitudes.
- Limitation: outcomes are almost all addiction scales (IAT, SAS-SV), not logged behaviour; parry2021 shows these barely track actual use.
- Limitation: clinical/therapeutic framing (addiction treatment); most studies from China, Korea and Iran in schools and universities; English only.
- Limitation: messy reporting: reference ranges broken in the text ("undefined"), a gaming study counted in the smartphone pooled analysis, and pre–post effects reported with positive signs while treatment–control effects are negative.

## How it relates to my thesis

Useful as the up-to-date overview of *therapeutic* interventions for the State of the Art, and as a contrast: almost all are therapist- or program-delivered (CBT, mindfulness courses, exercise), not app-based self-regulation tools, and only two smartphone-specific digital interventions exist, one with no advantage over control. That points to a gap between clinical treatment research and the HCI tools in lyngs2019 / mongeroffarello2019. Its findings that guided, practised programs beat unguided ones and that education needs active tasks and self-monitoring are relevant to any app-based intervention. It also repeats the measurement problem (self-report scales instead of logs, short follow-up), the same weakness noted in radtke2021.
