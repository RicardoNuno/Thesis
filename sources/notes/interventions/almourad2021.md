# almourad2021: Digital wellbeing tools through users lens

**Authors / venue / year:** Almourad, Alrobai, Skinner, Hussain & Ali / Technology in Society 67, 101778 / 2021
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.techsoc.2021.101778 (open access)
**Relevance:** low

## Summary (3 sentences)

Thematic analysis of 350 app-store reviews (195 of SPACE, a third-party phone-life balance app; 155 of Google Digital Wellbeing, GDW) to find what makes users accept or reject digital wellbeing apps, mapped onto the Technology Acceptance Model (usefulness and ease of use). Users liked usage awareness (tracking, reminders, charts, unlock counts, grayscale), goal setting and a sense of choice, a friendly non-judgemental tone, and simple design. They rejected the apps for inaccurate tracking, rigid settings (fixed midnight reset, no per-day limits, can't exclude work apps), reminders that became repetitive and were dismissed automatically, little visible effect, and, for GDW, being pre-installed and impossible to remove.

## Method

- 195 SPACE reviews (Google Play and App Store) and 155 GDW reviews (Google Play and website), filtered to "insightful" ones that give reasons; ratings ignored (often low because of fixable bugs).
- Two coders used each app for a week, then did thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke), producing themes → sub-themes → categories; mapped to TAM items (perceived usefulness and ease of use).
- No user demographics (not available from review platforms).

## Results / evidence

- **Acceptance (Table 1):** usage awareness (tracking, reminders, notifications, progress, charts, home-screen widget with unlocks and time); control (goal setting, "power of choice", gentle nudging); motivation (battery, sleep, time management); reported behaviour change (fewer unlocks, less aimless scrolling, deleting apps); friendly, humorous, non-intrusive language; ease of use and personalisation; GDW's grayscale mode ("helps me to be less addicted").
- **Rejection (Table 2):** lack of customisation (timer reset fixed at midnight, no day-of-week limits, can't exclude calls or work apps); unreliability (pause mode re-enabling itself, whitelisted apps still counted, wrong unlock counts, System UI crashes); performance below expectations (Wind Down still letting WhatsApp calls through); **desensitisation**: "There's not enough variety in the pop-ups so after a few days dismissing them becomes automatic"; little perceived effect or scepticism; trust: GDW pre-installed and not removable, unclear data collection.
- Technical bugs mostly in SPACE (third-party, OS and phone-model limits); trust and freedom-of-choice complaints mostly in GDW.
- Authors' conclusion: users need a balance between control and autonomy; tools should be designed as behavioural interventions, not utilities; OS restrictions cause many reliability issues ("software designs are not yet digital-wellbeing-native").

## Strengths / limitations

- Strength: compares a native OS tool with a third-party app and separates acceptance from rejection factors.
- Limitation: only two apps and 350 reviews, filtered subjectively; reviews are biased to extremes and give no demographics or context.
- Limitation: TAM mapping is post hoc; no measure of actual use or effect.
- Limitation: covers the same ground as mongeroffarello2019 (42 apps, 1,128 reviews, plus a field study), which is more comprehensive.
- Limitation: some loose writing (e.g. "DGW" for GDW; sections out of order in the published layout).

## How it relates to my thesis

Mostly confirms what mongeroffarello2019 found from a larger review set, so it adds little on its own. Useful specific points for a prototype compared against Digital Wellbeing: users want control over the reset time, day-specific limits and excluding work or navigation apps; reminders must vary or users habituate and dismiss them automatically (the same habituation issue as haliburton2024's fixed wait and kasturiratna2025's memorised passcode); tracking must be accurate (wrong unlock counts made people uninstall); and transparency about what is collected matters, especially for a native tool people can't remove. Also another source that OS restrictions cause reliability problems for third-party wellbeing apps.
