# olson2022nudge: A Nudge-Based Intervention to Reduce Problematic Smartphone Use: Randomised Controlled Trial

**Authors / venue / year:** Olson, Sandra, Chmoulevitch, Raz & Veissière / International Journal of Mental Health and Addiction 21(6), 3842–3864 / 2023 (online 2022)
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1007/s11469-022-00826-w (open access; PMC9112639)
**Relevance:** medium

## Summary (3 sentences)

Two pre-registered studies at McGill tested a "mix and match" intervention of ten non-app nudges (e.g. disable non-essential notifications, greyscale, password instead of Face/Touch ID, social apps hidden in a folder, phone out of reach at night). Study 1 (pre–post, N = 51) and Study 2 (randomised against a screen-time-tracking control, N = 70) both found lower problematic smartphone use (SAS-SV) and about an hour less daily screen time after 2 weeks; Study 2 also found better sleep quality, while depression and cognition effects from Study 1 did not replicate against the control. Participants still followed about 5.5 of the 10 strategies at 6 weeks, helped by many being "set and forget" settings changes.

## Method

- Participants: university students, 18–34, iPhone users only (iOS 12+ Screen Time); paid $20 or course credit.
- Baseline: 1 week of Screen Time tracking before the lab visit, so tracking itself is separated from the intervention.
- Intervention: the experimenter walks through ten strategies (Table 1) in ~10 min; participants choose which to follow and how strictly. Rationale grounded in the Fogg Behaviour Model (reduce prompts, ability, motivation).
- Study 1: pre–post over 2 weeks, plus an extra $20 for following the strategies. Measures: SAS-SV, Screen Time screenshots, BDI-II, sleep quality (one item), OSpan, SART, PANAS.
- Study 2: random assignment to intervention or control (Screen Time tracking only), with a cover story; 2-week confirmatory assessment and 6-week exploratory follow-up (only N = 44 at follow-up). Mixed-effects regressions on the condition × time interaction. Only participants following ≥5 strategies were analysed (4 excluded), and COVID-19 cut the sample from 80 to 70.

## Results / evidence

- Compliance: 7.5/10 strategies in Study 1; 8.8 agreed and 7.3 followed in Study 2; 5.6 at 6 weeks. Notifications most followed; greyscale (38% in Study 1) and leaving the phone at home least.
- SAS-SV, Study 1: 35.3 → 28.1 (d = −0.96); high-risk share 67% → 35%. Study 2: intervention −5.5 vs control −0.6 points.
- Screen time, Study 1: 4.67 → 3.40 h/day (d = −0.87). Study 2: −57 min vs −11 min; social networking −16 min vs +8 min.
- Sleep quality: no change in Study 1; Study 2 +1.23 vs −0.60 points, still present at 6 weeks but the gap narrowed.
- Depression and working memory improved in Study 1 but both groups improved equally in Study 2; no effect on SART or mood.
- Interviews (Study 2): more focus (23% vs 6%), less anxiety (21% vs 0%); only adverse effect was anxiety from missing conversations (5% vs 3%). Freed time went to study/work (51%), hobbies (26%), friends and family (21%), and only 13% to computers.
- Screen Time tracking alone had little effect on phone use.

## Strengths / limitations

- Strength: randomised, pre-registered, active control is the built-in screen time tool, and a 1-week tracking baseline separates tracking from intervention.
- Strength: reports compliance per strategy and what the freed time was used for.
- Limitation: a bundle where participants pick components, so no single strategy's effect can be isolated (the authors call it a "shotgun" approach).
- Limitation: Study 2 analysed only participants who followed ≥5 strategies (per-protocol), and the groups differed at baseline screen time.
- Limitation: screen time comes from participant-sent iOS screenshots; iPhone-only students; mostly women; small samples; COVID disruption.
- Limitation: Study 1 used no correction for six tests and paid participants to comply.

## How it relates to my thesis

Evidence that changing existing phone settings, without a dedicated app, beats screen time tracking, which is the same comparison my proposal makes against Digital Wellbeing. Several strategies map directly to Android features (notification controls, Bedtime mode greyscale, app folders, disabling biometric unlock), and the low compliance with greyscale and the anxiety from muted notifications are acceptability data worth noting. The password-instead-of-biometrics strategy is the friction-at-unlock idea from kim2019lockntype in its simplest form, and the authors' suggestion of an OS setting that "make[s] the phone unlock slightly slower" connects to haliburton2024. Its weakness is the same as many studies here: 2 weeks of main data and self-sent screenshots rather than logging (cf. parry2021).
