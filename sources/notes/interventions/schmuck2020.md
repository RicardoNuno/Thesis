# schmuck2020: Does Digital Detox Work? Exploring the Role of Digital Detox Applications for Problematic Smartphone Use and Well-Being of Young Adults Using Multigroup Analysis

**Authors / venue / year:** Desirée Schmuck / Cyberpsychology, Behavior, and Social Networking 23(8), 526–532 / 2020
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1089/cyber.2019.0578 (accepted manuscript: https://lirias.kuleuven.be/retrieve/abcd8d97-2e8a-4bdf-a35d-35ba6802a938)
**Relevance:** medium

_Skimmed from the KU Leuven accepted manuscript, so wording and numbers may differ slightly from the published version; quotes cite sections._

## Summary (3 sentences)

Cross-sectional online survey of 500 Austrian young adults (18–35) comparing people who had ever used a "digital detox app" (iOS Screen Time, Android Digital Wellbeing, Forest, Moment, etc.) with people who had not. Among non-users, more mobile social media use went with more problematic smartphone use (PSU), which went with lower life satisfaction; among users, neither link was present. The author reads this, through Bandura's self-regulation theory, as detox apps buffering the harms of social media, while acknowledging the design cannot show causation.

## Method

- Online survey, May/June 2019, recruited through the university network; N = 500 (mean age 22.4, 58% women, mostly students with a high school degree or more). Ten €15 vouchers raffled.
- Mobile social media use: self-estimated daily minutes on a 0–360 slider for Facebook, Instagram, Snapchat, YouTube and WhatsApp, averaged into an index.
- PSU: 4 items (α = .69). Well-being: 4 items of the Satisfaction with Life Scale (α = .80).
- Detox app use: binary, "ever used" any listed app (asked at the end of the questionnaire).
- Covariates: gender, age, education, living situation, self-discipline, perceived social pressure to reply, general smartphone use.
- SEM in lavaan, then multi-group analysis (users vs non-users); bootstrapped indirect effects.

## Results / evidence

- 41.6% had used a detox app: iOS Screen Time 31.2%, Forest 4.4%, Android Digital Wellbeing 2.4%, Moment 1.2%, others ≤1% each.
- Users and non-users did not differ in education, general phone use, social pressure or self-discipline; women and older participants were more likely to be users.
- Whole sample: social media use → PSU (β = .19) and PSU → well-being (β = −.20); detox app use moderated the first path (interaction β = −.18).
- Detox app users reported *higher* PSU (β = .14) and higher well-being (β = .12) directly.
- Non-users: social media → PSU β = .30; PSU → well-being β = −.32; significant indirect effect. Users: β = .00 and β = −.07, neither significant.

## Strengths / limitations

- Strength: names Android Digital Wellbeing alongside third-party apps, and gives a usage share for each in 2019; controls for self-discipline and social pressure.
- Limitation: cross-sectional, so "detox apps prevent harm" is not supported; users may differ in ways not measured (the higher PSU among users suggests self-selection by people who already see a problem).
- Limitation: "ever used" a detox app counts someone who once opened Screen Time the same as a daily user of blocking, although parry2023 shows most use is passive report-glancing.
- Limitation: all variables self-reported, including minutes of use (cf. parry2021); short scales with modest reliability (α = .69).
- Limitation: the discussion says users "indicated lower levels of perceived PSU", but the results show users reported *higher* PSU; the finding is a weaker association, not a lower level.
- Limitation: well-being measured only as life satisfaction, with no negative-affect or depression measure; one country, mostly students.

## How it relates to my thesis

Gives the "detox app" framing and shows how small Android Digital Wellbeing's share was in 2019 compared with Screen Time (2.4% vs 31.2%), which matches parry2023. The result is at most a correlation, so it supports interest in such tools rather than their effectiveness; olson2022nudge and haliburton2024 are better evidence for that. Its scope argument helps justify a smartphone-focused thesis: smartphones give constant access to social media and are where most detox tools live, while other devices, including smartwatches, are left as future work.
