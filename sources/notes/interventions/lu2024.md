# lu2024: InteractOut: Leveraging Interaction Proxies as Input Manipulation Strategies for Reducing Smartphone Overuse

**Authors / venue / year:** Lu, Zheng, Zhang, Xu & Guo / CHI '24 / 2024
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1145/3613904.3642317 (preprint: https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.16668)
**Relevance:** high

_Skimmed from the arXiv v2 preprint (Feb 2024); quotes cite sections._

## Summary (3 sentences)

Instead of changing the screen's output (lock screens, notifications), InteractOut changes the user's input once a daily limit is reached: taps and swipes are delayed, shifted or slowed, through a transparent Accessibility overlay that catches gestures and replays modified ones with `dispatchGesture`. A lab study (N = 30) picked four techniques (Tap Delay 1000 ms, Tap Shift 200 dp, Swipe Delay 800 ms, Swipe Deceleration ×0.25), and a 5-week within-subject field study (N = 42 Android users) compared them with a timed lockout. On participant-chosen target apps, InteractOut cut usage time and opens more than lockout (normalised usage 0.78 vs 0.94) and was accepted, not bypassed, more often (61.6% vs 36.3%), although overall phone use did not drop significantly and workload was higher.

## Method

- Design space: 4 gesture properties (time, location, direction, number of fingers) × 4 strategies (shift, complicate, extend, disable) → 12 types; 8 implemented (Tap Delay, Tap Prolong, Tap Shift, Tap Double, Swipe Delay, Swipe Deceleration, Swipe Reverse, Swipe Multiple Fingers). Lockout and "temporal disable" fit in the same space.
- Implementation (Android): Accessibility Service with a transparent `TYPE_ACCESSIBILITY_OVERLAY` as proxy layer; a custom gesture detector reads duration, location/trajectory and finger count; `dispatchGesture` performs the modified gesture. App usage time is also monitored via the Accessibility API. Persistent notification shows the active intervention and offers bypass options ("One more minute", "15 more minutes", "Ignore for today"), copied from iOS Screen Time.
- Intensity ramps up: each touch (or each idle minute) adds a step (10 ms for delays), saturating after ~80–100 touches; Tap Shift is fixed.
- Lab study: 30 students (SAS-SV ≥ 30 and wanting to change), Pixel 5a, Twitter (swipes) and Bubble Mania (taps), each technique at two intensities for 3 min; Likert ratings and rankings.
- Field study (Mar–Apr 2023): 42 students, own Android phones (mostly Samsung, Pixel). Baseline week, then two counterbalanced 12-day periods (InteractOut with one tap + one swipe technique, or lockout), each followed by 2 break days. Fixed 1 h/day budget across chosen target apps (Instagram 25, YouTube 18, Reddit 10…). Logged app enter/exit, interventions and bypasses to Firebase; weekly NASA-TLX; exit interviews. Paid up to $100.
- Analysis: usage normalised to each participant's baseline; GLMMs with Tukey post-hoc tests.

## Results / evidence

- Lab: all techniques were more annoying and demanding than none; higher intensities were preferred because low ones went unnoticed ("500ms delay is too short that I could not even tell", P21). Techniques with high mental but low physical load (Tap Shift, delays) were favoured; Swipe Reverse was learned within a few tries; multi-finger swipes were physically awkward.
- Target-app usage time per day: baseline 149 min, lockout 131 min, InteractOut 104 min; InteractOut 15.6% lower than lockout. The four technique combinations performed similarly.
- Target-app opens: baseline 66, lockout 57 (not significantly different from baseline), InteractOut 48.
- All apps combined: no significant change in usage time; people moved to non-target apps (after an intervention, 51% went to a non-target app, 27% to another target app, 22% turned the screen off).
- Effect depends on how much the app is touched: no advantage over lockout for low-interaction apps (video, e-books); games requiring precision became unplayable.
- Break days: opens stayed lower, usage time returned to baseline, for both conditions.
- Acceptance (not bypassed): 61.6% vs 36.3%. NASA-TLX workload higher with InteractOut.
- Interviews: liked being able to finish the current task, a constant reminder, and losing interest as scrolling felt sluggish; some changed how they saw the app itself. Five participants felt it was ineffective while their logs showed reduced use.

## Strengths / limitations

- Strength: novel, clearly described Android implementation with a systematic design space; logged rather than self-reported use; compared against the common lockout design; counterbalanced order.
- Strength: reports substitution to other apps and the effect by app type rather than only headline numbers.
- Limitation: 12 days per condition and 2 break days, so habituation and long-term effects are unknown.
- Limitation: university students who wanted to change and scored high on SAS-SV; mostly men.
- Limitation: the lockout baseline is the authors' own, not Android Digital Wellbeing; time budget and techniques were fixed by researchers.
- Limitation: headline numbers differ within the paper (abstract 15.6%/16.5%/25.3% vs introduction 15.0%/17.0%/25.4%; acceptance 61.6% vs 61.3%), and the comparison with LocknType, okeke2018 and TypeOut uses numbers from different studies.
- Limitation: relies on an Accessibility Service, which Google restricts for non-accessibility use; the authors note this and the risk of the technique being seen as a dark pattern or a device malfunction. Battery impact is not reported.
- Limitation: swipe effects only appear after the finger is lifted, because Android offers no API to alter motion events live.

## How it relates to my thesis

The most detailed Android implementation of in-app friction in my reading so far, naming the exact APIs (Accessibility overlay, `dispatchGesture`, usage via Accessibility events) and their limits, so it maps directly onto the technical survey part of my proposal. It adds a third kind of friction alongside blocking (kim2019goalkeeper), pre-access tasks (kim2019lockntype) and fixed delays (haliburton2024): degrading interaction while use continues, and ramping up to resist habituation. Its results reinforce recurring themes: soft friction is accepted more than lockouts, effects fade without the intervention, and time saved in target apps leaks to other apps, so evaluations should log whole-phone use. The Accessibility dependency also ties to the Play Store policy issue that makes such apps hard to distribute, and the lack of battery data leaves room for my evaluation criteria.
