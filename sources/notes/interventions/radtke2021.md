# radtke2021: Digital detox: An effective solution in the smartphone era? A systematic literature review

**Authors / venue / year:** Radtke, Apel, Schenkel, Keller & von Lindern / Mobile Media & Communication 10(2), 190–215 / 2022 (online 2021)
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1177/20501579211028647 (open access)
**Relevance:** medium

## Summary (3 sentences)

PRISMA systematic review of 21 field studies (N = 3,625, 12 RCTs) in which people voluntarily took a timeout from their smartphone, social media or messaging, for 24 hours to 4 weeks. Detox consistently reduced usage during the intervention, and the three studies measuring depression all found fewer symptoms; but life satisfaction, affect, stress, loneliness, FoMO and social connectedness gave mixed or even negative results, performance showed no effect, and craving and withdrawal went up. The authors conclude there is no clear answer yet and call for larger, device-measured, longer studies that test for whom and why detox helps, and for interventions that promote healthy use rather than abstinence.

## Method

- Searched 7 databases (PsycINFO, PBSC, PsycArticles, PubMed, Cochrane, Web of Science, Google Scholar) for 2008–Sep 2020, plus reference lists and key journals.
- Inclusion: English; voluntary, time-limited abstinence from the smartphone or a subset (social network sites, instant messaging); inferential comparison with a control group or baseline. Excluded: imposed restrictions (school, parents, employers), lab studies with cover stories, combined interventions (e.g. mindfulness), gaming disorder, qualitative studies.
- Two reviewers screened and rated quality (Kmet checklist; ICC = .95). No meta-analysis; results tabulated and categorised as +, −, ± or no effect.
- Proposes extending the definition of digital detox: voluntary, intentional timeouts from devices, but also from specific apps, brands, features, interactions or messages (Meier & Reinecke's taxonomy).

## Results / evidence

- Studies: 11 countries (US 7); mostly students (15 of 21); sample sizes 15–1,095 (median 98). Content: social media 13 (Facebook 6), whole smartphone 5, all devices 1, texting 1. Duration: mostly 1 week (9); 7 shorter, 4 longer.
- Quality: none below 55%, 6 above 75% (high).
- **Usage:** all studies measuring it (6 device-based, 3 self-report) found less use, medium effects.
- **Depression:** all 3 studies found a decline.
- **Performance:** no effect on cognitive/physical performance or GPA (3 studies); one found more time distortion after a Facebook break.
- **Self-control:** mixed; NUGU (ko2015, group detox) showed no gain in self-efficacy; procrastination fell after Facebook breaks.
- **Life satisfaction:** 4 up, 2 no effect, 2 down. **Affect:** mostly no effect; 2 found more negative affect, 2 benefits.
- **Stress:** 3 of 5 found reductions (incl. cortisol, skin conductance), mainly for heavy users or those with mild–moderate symptoms. **Anxiety:** 1 of 4 found a reduction.
- **Craving/withdrawal:** increased in all 3 studies that measured it; boredom and social pressure also rose.
- **Social:** FoMO, loneliness and connectedness all mixed (some up, some down).
- Why mixed: different instructions (self-set vs total abstinence), measures and timing, sample types; few follow-ups; adherence rarely checked (only 3 studies physically locked away the phone); compensatory use of other devices/apps not controlled.
- Recommendations: large, randomised field studies with device-based measures, longer periods and follow-ups; test moderators (addiction level, usage, gender) and mediators; interventions that promote healthy use rather than discourage use.

## Strengths / limitations

- Strength: systematic PRISMA review with dual screening and quality rating; clear outcome-by-outcome overview; useful refinement of what "digital detox" means.
- Limitation: no meta-analysis (vote counting), so effect sizes aren't pooled; mostly small student samples and short timeouts.
- Limitation: **timing of measurement**: most outcomes were measured during the timeout or right after it (mostly one week, none longer than 4 weeks, few follow-ups), i.e. at the peak of withdrawal. Negative results (craving, boredom, FoMO, lower life satisfaction) may partly reflect withdrawal rather than the effect of using the phone less, and positive results may be novelty that fades; short studies can't show whether people adapt.
- Limitation: English-only, Western-dominated; search terms may miss studies using other wording.
- Limitation: minor reporting slips: "eight non-randomized" trials where 21 − 12 = 9; cites "Ko et al., 2018" for usage where the table lists Ko et al., 2015; seven studies said to assess affect but eight are cited.

## How it relates to my thesis

The evidence on full abstinence is inconclusive rather than negative: it cuts usage while it lasts, but the reported craving, withdrawal, boredom and FoMO were measured during or right after short timeouts, so they may say more about the first days of withdrawal than about whether abstinence works. What it does show is that short detoxes are hard to sustain and uncomfortable, which is a practical argument for interventions that shape *how* people use the phone (e.g. awareness, friction, regret prompts) rather than removing it. Its own recommendation is in the same direction: "emphasize a healthy way of using the smartphone, instead of discouraging the use". Methodologically it repeats the call for device-based measurement and checking adherence, and its extended definition (timeouts from specific apps or features, not just the device) matches the app/feature-level focus from cho2021 and mongeroffarello2019. Also covers ko2015 (NUGU), already on my list. The short timeframe is also a lesson for my own evaluation: measuring well-being only during or right after an intervention captures the adjustment period, not the lasting effect, so I should plan a longer exposure and a follow-up after it ends. The same weakness applies to the intervention studies I've read (kim2019goalkeeper one week per condition, mongeroffarello2019 and kim2017pomodolock two weeks).
