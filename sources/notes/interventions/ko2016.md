# ko2016: Lock n' LoL: Group-based Limiting Assistance App to Mitigate Smartphone Distractions in Group Activities

**Authors / venue / year:** Ko, Choi, Yatani & Lee / CHI '16, 998–1010 / 2016
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1145/2858036.2858568
**Relevance:** medium

## Summary (3 sentences)

Lock n' LoL is an Android app that lets a group (friends studying together, a couple at dinner, a club) lock their phones at the same time: members join a "room", notifications are muted, everyone sees who is locked, and each person can unlock alone for 5 minutes per hour or ask the group for permission after that. A campus-wide campaign at a Korean university (976 users, 10,365 hours of limiting) showed lower self-reported distraction afterwards, with users crediting peer pressure, awareness of habitual checking, and better conversations. The 5-minute allowance was treated as a "precious resource" and usually used for under a minute, showing that a little flexibility makes group restriction acceptable.

## Method

- Preliminary survey (n = 87) and 7 interviews: 77% had experienced phone distraction in group activities; 90.8% thought use should be limited; distractions came from notifications and from unconsciously picking up the phone.
- Two pilot iterations (n = 20, n = 28) shaped the design: incoming calls allowed; self-unlock first required permission, then 5 min per session, finally 5 min recharged every hour; invites via KakaoTalk; co-location reminder.
- Features: personal stats and timeline; group limit mode (room, activity type, members' status, notifications muted); temporary unlimit; Wi-Fi-based co-location reminder (Jaccard similarity of Wi-Fi scans ≥ 0.6 ≈ within 20 m, max one reminder per place per 6 h).
- Field deployment: app on Google Play, campus campaign with posters and incentives (gift voucher for the first 300 users reaching 90 min of limiting; prize draw). 976 registered users (mean age 22.6, 37% female, mostly students). Logs of app interactions only; pre/post Smartphone Distraction Scale (5 items); exit survey (n = 379); 61 user stories. Regression of engagement on activity, relationship, time and place variables.

## Results / evidence

- Distraction score fell from 3.30 to 2.36 (d ≈ 0.99, n = 379 who completed both).
- Mean limiting 91 min/user/day; 44.6% limited for ≥ 2 h in total.
- Activities: studying 80% of sessions (~99 min); eating/chatting ~40 min; "etc." (clubs, movies, exercise) longest at 200 min.
- 74% of limits were with others, mostly groups of 2–3; friends 53%, couples 17%, club members 17%. Surprisingly, 76% of group limits were remote (members in different places).
- Group limit mode rated most helpful (57.7%): made people notice their habitual checking; peer pressure ("I feel awkward if I use the phone alone"); seeing others studying kept them going; more eye contact and attention in conversation.
- Temporary unlimit rated most helpful by 33.5%: used in 44% of sessions, 2.4 times per session, mean 50 s; mostly email (52%), messages (19%), browsing (16%); asking others for permission happened only 3.1% of the time.
- Engagement predictors: duration linked to regular schedules (classes, mornings, club and family members, co-location); frequency linked to diversity of activities, relationships and places.
- Problems with power distance: a teacher forced students to use it; a graduate student felt pressured by seniors/advisors.

## Strengths / limitations

- Strength: very large real-world deployment; iterative design with documented reasons for each flexibility choice.
- Strength: shows social commitment as an alternative to individual willpower or hard blocking.
- Limitation: **no logging of overall phone use** (excluded for privacy), so effectiveness rests on a self-reported distraction scale and on in-app logs; no control group.
- Limitation: incentives tied to limiting time and a campaign framing may inflate use and post-test scores; only 39% completed the exit survey.
- Limitation: mostly students at one technical university in Korea; the 5-item distraction scale was adapted ad hoc.
- Limitation: says the campaign lasted 25 days but gives dates from 26 May to 19 July 2015.

## How it relates to my thesis

Adds the social dimension to the intervention taxonomy (lyngs2019's reward/punishment and social sharing): peer pressure and shared commitment can stand in for hard blocking. Its flexibility design is directly reusable: a small, self-managed allowance (5 min per hour) made restriction acceptable and was used sparingly, which echoes kim2019goalkeeper's 15-min allowances and kim2017pomodolock's stoppable sessions. Also a technical reference for Android context detection (Wi-Fi fingerprint co-location) and for muting notifications during a session. Less central if the prototype is individual rather than group-based, and the study didn't log actual usage.
