# panova2018: Is smartphone addiction really an addiction?

**Authors / venue / year:** Panova & Carbonell / Journal of Behavioral Addictions 7(2), 252–259 / 2018
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1556/2006.7.2018.49
**Relevance:** high

## Summary (3 sentences)

A critical review that checks smartphone "addiction" against the standard criteria for addiction. The authors conclude it does not meet them: the harms are not severe enough and the studies are weak. They propose dropping the word "addiction" and using "problematic smartphone use" instead.

## Method

- Narrative critical review, no systematic search. The authors go through the addiction criteria one by one and argue whether smartphone use meets each.
- Criteria used: functional impairment, physical consequences, tolerance, salience, withdrawal, loss of control, stability over time, relapse, "better explained by" another condition (Griffiths' components model, DSM-5, ICD-11 draft, Kardefelt-Winther et al. 2017).

## Results / evidence

Their argument, criterion by criterion:

- **Functional impairment:** academic and relationship problems exist but can have other causes.
- **Physical consequences:** only "mild tendinitis" and "anecdotal" reports of blurred vision, wrist and neck pain, and texting-while-walking accidents (p. 254).
- **Tolerance:** "more time on the phone" could mean a promotion or new friends, not addiction. Items like "same satisfaction as before" are vague.
- **Salience:** thinking about the phone is reasonable because so much of life runs through it (p. 254).
- **Withdrawal:** stress without the phone is normal for an essential tool, and also comes from fear of theft or damage.
- **Loss of control:** below clinical severity; a high Likert score is not clinically relevant.
- **Stability / relapse:** no longitudinal or clinical studies, so no evidence either way.
- **Better explained by:** the problems may come from other conditions (anxiety, attachment style).
- **Screening studies:** healthy student samples, scales not valid for diagnosis, many false positives, arbitrary items, self-report, inconsistent cut-offs.
- **Smartphone as an object:** the phone is like the glass in alcohol addiction; the addiction is to activities on it (social media, games, gambling), not to the device.
- **Conclusion:** use "problematic use" instead of "addiction", and study the behaviour through motivations and gratifications.

## Strengths / limitations

- Strength: valid criticism of the screening studies (self-report, student samples, scales borrowed from substance addiction, no clinical samples).
- Strength: gives a clear, accepted reason to use "problematic smartphone use" instead of "addiction".
- Limitation: a position paper, not a neutral review. The conclusion is set in the introduction ("With all the above in mind", p. 253) and the criteria are then argued towards it.
- Limitation: calls physical harms "anecdotal" while citing a single case letter (Fernandez-Guerrero 2014). Systematic and epidemiological evidence on neck and musculoskeletal pain and pedestrian injuries exists (see below).
- Limitation: the "necessity" argument (salience and withdrawal are normal for an essential tool) proves too much. Necessary things (food, work, sex) can still become compulsive problems, and the phone is a necessity only for some tasks, not most of the time it is used.
- Limitation: the "object" argument treats the phone as a container only. It ignores device-level habits that cut across apps: compulsive checking, notification-driven pickups, unlocking with no goal.
- Limitation: says there are no standard diagnostic criteria but does not discuss Lin et al. (2016), who proposed criteria validated with psychiatrist interviews.

## How it relates to my thesis

Justifies the term "problematic smartphone use" over "addiction". The thesis does not need the clinical claim: it needs "people use the phone more than they want and it causes problems", which this paper accepts. Its own conclusion also supports a device-level intervention: the phone's portability and convenience "facilitate the access" to problem behaviours (p. 257), which is what moving functions to a smartwatch addresses.

## Evidence against the paper

**Physical harm (against "anecdotal"):**

- Eitivipart, Viriyarojanakul & Redhead (2018). *Musculoskeletal disorder and pain associated with smartphone use: A systematic review of biomechanical evidence.* Hong Kong Physiotherapy Journal 38, 77–90. https://doi.org/10.1142/S1013702518300010. 12 studies: smartphone use increases neck-extensor and upper-trapezius muscle activity, head flexion and forward head posture.
- Mustafaoglu, Yasaci, Zirek, Griffiths & Ozdincler (2021). *The relationship between smartphone addiction and musculoskeletal pain prevalence among young population: a cross-sectional study.* Korean Journal of Pain 34(1), 72–81. https://doi.org/10.3344/kjp.2021.34.1.72. 249 students: higher SAS scores associated with neck, wrist/hand and upper-back pain.
- Nasar & Troyer (2013). *Pedestrian injuries due to mobile phone use in public places.* Accident Analysis & Prevention 57, 91–95. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.aap.2013.03.021. US emergency-room data 2004–2010: phone-related pedestrian injuries more than doubled since 2005 while total pedestrian injuries fell.

**Addiction criteria (against "not an addiction"):**

- Lin, Chiang, Lin, Chang, Ko, Lee & Lin (2016). *Proposed Diagnostic Criteria for Smartphone Addiction.* PLOS ONE 11(11), e0163010. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0163010. Criteria validated against psychiatrist structured interviews (281 students), 84.3% accuracy, with functional-impairment and exclusion criteria. Counters "no standardized criteria" and "no clinical assessment".
- Eide, Aarestad, Andreassen, Bilder & Pallesen (2018). *Smartphone Restriction and Its Effect on Subjective Withdrawal Related Scores.* Frontiers in Psychology 9, 1444. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.01444. Randomised 72-hour restriction: higher withdrawal and FoMO scores than controls. Counters the withdrawal argument.
- Jameel, Shahnawaz & Griffiths (2019). *Smartphone addiction in students: A qualitative examination of the components model of addiction using face-to-face interviews.* Journal of Behavioral Addictions 8(4), 780–793. https://doi.org/10.1556/2006.8.2019.57. Interviews instead of scales: "addicted" users differed from non-addicted on salience, tolerance, withdrawal and conflict. Small sample (12); no relapse found.
- Horvath et al. (2020). *Structural and functional correlates of smartphone addiction.* Addictive Behaviors 105, 106334. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.addbeh.2020.106334. MRI: lower grey matter in insula and other regions and lower ACC activity in the addiction group, as in other addictions. Small sample (22 vs 26), cross-sectional.

None of these is a direct reply to the paper, and none settles the clinical-severity point; they answer specific criteria.

## Quotes / figures worth citing (with page)

- "we did not find sufficient support from the addiction perspective to confirm the existence of smartphone addiction at this time" (Abstract, p. 252)
- "A behavior may have a similar presentation as addiction in terms of excessive use, impulse control problems, and negative consequences, but that does not mean that it should be considered an addiction." (Abstract, p. 252)
