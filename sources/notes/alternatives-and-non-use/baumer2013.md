# baumer2013: Limiting, Leaving, and (Re)lapsing: An Exploration of Facebook Non-Use Practices and Experiences

**Authors / venue / year:** Baumer, Adams, Khovanskaya, Liao, Smith, Schwanda Sosik & Williams / CHI '13, 3257–3266 / 2013
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1145/2470654.2466446 (data and questionnaire: http://hdl.handle.net/1813/30908)
**Relevance:** medium

## Summary (3 sentences)

Online questionnaire (N = 410, June–July 2012) asking Facebook users, leavers and never-users to tell stories about leaving or limiting Facebook. Use and non-use are not a binary: people limit (blocking add-ons, redirecting notifications to a junk inbox, a partner changing the password), deactivate temporarily, delete, and relapse, and many "lag" in resisting, wanting to quit but not doing so. Motivations were privacy, data misuse by Facebook, banality, productivity, addiction-like experiences and external pressures (work, exes, institutions), and leaving also affects the friends left behind.

## Method

- Questionnaire adapted from story-eliciting surveys: factual yes/no and Likert items on use, deactivation, deletion and consideration of leaving, plus open-ended stories ("a time when you or someone you knew left Facebook or systematically limited their use"); branching by respondent status.
- Recruited via academic mailing lists (AoIR, CHI) and snowball sampling on Facebook and Twitter; ~500 responses, 410 kept (18+). 41% academics, 13% tech-savvy; ages 19–76 (median 33); mostly US and Europe.
- Open coding of free-text answers by question, then by respondent, with a second coder confirming; theme frequencies given only as rough proportions.

## Results / evidence

- 26.8% had deactivated (66% happy with it), 11.2% had deleted (91% happy, none very unhappy); 75 never had an account; 59 left and came back.
- Knowing someone who deactivated made respondents ~3× more likely to deactivate (χ² = 14.3, p < .001); knowing a deleter did not affect deleting but increased considering it.
- Practices: never joining, deactivating (e.g. every exam period), deleting, returning with restrictions (e.g. a "research-contacts only" account, unfriending hundreds), limiting via browser blockers or redirects, emails sent to an unused inbox, a spouse holding the password ("he'd log me on once a week or so as a treat").
- Deletion was hard to find and confused with deactivation; Facebook's deactivation screen shows friends who "will miss you".
- Motivations (rough shares): privacy (>¼), data use and misuse by Facebook (~⅕), productivity (~⅕, strongly linked to academics), banality (~⅙), external pressures (~1/7: students, exes, employers, parole), addiction-like withdrawal, shaming and envy of quitters (~1/10).
- New concept "lagging resistance": many considered leaving but hadn't, because of advisors or groups requiring Facebook, fear of isolation, or seeing quitters as unattainable ideals. Non-use can be stigmatised ("if something was wrong"), and pressures can force use as well as non-use.
- Design suggestion: offer more varied "self-inhibiting" options beyond deactivation and deletion, while doubting Facebook's interest in doing so.

## Strengths / limitations

- Strength: early, influential mapping of the space between use and non-use, with concrete self-limiting practices and an open dataset.
- Limitation: self-selected sample heavily skewed to academics, likely over-representing privacy and productivity motives and people opposed to Facebook.
- Limitation: survey stories without follow-up; theme proportions are rough and not representative; no usage data.
- Limitation: about Facebook on desktop in 2012, before smartphones dominated access; minor count inconsistency (110 deactivated + 46 deleted − 28 both = 128, reported as 127).

## How it relates to my thesis

Shows that disengagement is a spectrum (limit, pause, leave, relapse) rather than an on/off choice, which supports designing interventions with graded, temporary and reversible options rather than only blocking (cf. lu2024, haliburton2024, kim2017pomodolock's user control). The self-made tricks people used (a partner holding the password, redirecting notifications, deactivating during exams) are low-tech versions of features later built into apps such as lockouts, scheduled downtime and notification batching. "Lagging resistance" and the social pressure to stay connected help explain why people who want to cut down often don't, which matches lee2026's finding that motivation drives outcomes and rothschild2024's costs of opting out. It also notes that platforms have little interest in helping people leave, the conflict of interest raised elsewhere (olson2022nudge, vanvelthoven2018).
