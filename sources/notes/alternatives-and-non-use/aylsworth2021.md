# aylsworth2021: Is There a Duty to Be a Digital Minimalist?

**Authors / venue / year:** Aylsworth & Castro / Journal of Applied Philosophy 38(4), 662–673 / 2021
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1111/japp.12498 (preprint: https://philpapers.org/rec/AYLITA)
**Relevance:** low

_Skimmed from the PhilArchive pre-production copy; quotes cite sections._

## Summary (3 sentences)

Philosophy paper arguing, from Kantian premises, that we have an imperfect moral duty to be "digital minimalists", that is, to use smartphones intentionally so that our use does not conflict with our own chosen ends. Smartphones are designed as habit-forming "Skinner boxes" (Eyal's Hook cycle: trigger, action, variable reward, investment) that capture attention, interrupt tasks and weaken the capacity to set and pursue ends, which is autonomy. Because rational agency has dignity, we must respect it in ourselves, so protecting it against "technological heteronomy" is a moral duty, not only a matter of self-interest, though how much and how to reduce use is left to discretion.

## Method

- Conceptual argument, no empirical work. Section 2 motivates the threat to autonomy with existing literature and popular sources; section 3 defends Kantian imperfect duties to oneself; section 4 links them to digital minimalism.
- Argument: P1 humanity (rational agency) has dignity; P2 dignity must be respected as an end; P3 so we have an imperfect duty to cultivate and protect our rational agency (C1); P4 if so, we ought to adopt the end of digital minimalism; C2 we ought to be digital minimalists.

## Results / evidence

- Redefines digital minimalism more loosely than Newport: not a small set of optimised activities or a full "philosophy of technology use", just a disposition to use technology intentionally so it does not conflict with one's ends; for most this means serious reduction, not abstinence.
- Autonomy failure illustrated across models (Frankfurt/Dworkin higher-order desires, Watson, Bratman long-term plans): the comedian who opens a book and "black[s] out" on Instagram.
- Smartphone addiction differs from coffee or cigarettes because it repeatedly hijacks attention and interrupts ongoing thought.
- Intentional use can enhance autonomy (e.g., an exercise app using streak notifications for a chosen goal), but autonomous use can drift into compulsive use "on the developers' terms".
- Claims other theories (consequentialism, egoism, virtue ethics) would likely reach a similar conclusion.

## Strengths / limitations

- Strength: clear normative framing of smartphone overuse as an autonomy problem, with a usable definition of digital minimalism.
- Limitation: no data; the empirical case leans on popular and market sources (Eyal's *Hooked*, Price, an Asurion survey of 96 unlocks a day, a BrainSharper survey) alongside academic ones.
- Limitation: takes "addiction" at face value, which much of the research literature disputes (cf. the problematic-use debate in the dependency notes).
- Limitation: places the duty on individuals, with little on designers' or platforms' responsibility.

## How it relates to my thesis

Mainly a citation for the ethical framing: overuse matters because it undermines autonomy, and the goal of an intervention is intentional use aligned with the user's own goals rather than abstinence. That aligns with the user-control theme (kim2017pomodolock, lyngs2019) and with designing interventions that serve self-chosen ends, as in its streak-notification example. Its point that autonomously chosen use can drift into compulsive use helps justify interventions at the moment of use (friction, nudges) rather than one-off settings. It adds no evidence about which interventions work.
