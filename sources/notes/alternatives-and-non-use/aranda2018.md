# aranda2018: Toward "JOMO": The Joy of Missing Out and the Freedom of Disconnecting

**Authors / venue / year:** Aranda & Baig (Google) / MobileHCI '18, Article 19 / 2018
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1145/3229434.3229468
**Relevance:** medium

## Summary (3 sentences)

Google Android researchers ran an ethnographic study (interviews, shadowing, group discussions) with 19 people in Zurich and the San Francisco Bay Area, plus two mindfulness coaches and a re-analysis of 112 participants from earlier studies, to understand excessive smartphone use and what disconnecting feels like. They describe two negative cycles, an internal habit cycle (trigger, action, variable reward; "pocket slot machine") and an external cycle of social obligation to respond, and a 2×2 taxonomy of disconnection by duration (short/long) and control (voluntary/forced). Short-term voluntary disconnection was the most acceptable and likely to succeed, so they recommend tools that facilitate disconnection, reduce temptation to re-engage with overridable obstacles, and allow partial disconnection that keeps essential functions.

## Method

- Qualitative mixed methods: in-home semi-structured interviews, full- and half-day shadowing, two-hour group discussions with friends or family; Zurich and SF Bay Area.
- 19 participants (11 women, 8 men, aged 18–65, several nationalities, Android and iOS), recruited across attitudes from satisfied to actively cutting down; plus two mindfulness professionals.
- Affinity diagramming and synthesis; retroactive analysis of 2015–2016 ethnographic data (112 participants in Japan, China, Singapore, US).
- Stated goal: "to make product recommendations to our business organization".

## Results / evidence

- Phones are hard to give up because they are indispensable (communication, navigation, transport) and have absorbed alarm clocks, cameras, cash, reference books; lulls feel like boredom ("Without my phone, what would I do— just stare out the window?!").
- Habit cycle: triggers range from intentional (checking the time) to unintentional (notification sound, boredom, FOMO); "The less intentional the action, the more regret the user expressed later"; phantom cues, infinite scroll, refreshing for new content.
- Obligation cycle: senders get anxious waiting for replies; receivers feel they must answer within ~20 minutes to the end of the day; workarounds include reading previews without opening and turning off read receipts.
- Taxonomy of disconnection: short-term voluntary (vacations, weekends, focus time; easiest), long-term voluntary (lifestyle change, boundaries; friends frustrated), short-term forced (lost/dead phone: anxiety, then sometimes relief and "freedom"), long-term forced (low-connectivity regions; not observed here).
- From FOMO to JOMO: after accepting a forced break people felt freer, partly because being unavailable was not their fault.
- Recommendations: (1) facilitate disconnection through reflection (usage metrics, reminders), support (accountability partners) and practice (user-defined limits by app, time window or contact); (2) reduce temptation to re-engage with a short lockout that can be overridden, a "speed bump"; (3) partial disconnection keeping dialer, maps, camera and clock while restricting other apps. Notes that OS-level features (notifications, volume, lockscreen) act before the user enters an app.

## Strengths / limitations

- Strength: written by the Android team that year, so it shows the reasoning behind the OS-level approach to digital wellbeing; rich observational methods (shadowing, groups).
- Strength: clear, usable taxonomy and design directions.
- Limitation: industry research aimed at product recommendations, by authors whose employer profits from phone engagement; no conflict-of-interest discussion.
- Limitation: small sample in two wealthy regions; methods and analysis briefly described; no quantitative data; the 112-participant re-analysis is barely reported.
- Limitation: short paper (MobileHCI); recommendations are not evaluated.

## How it relates to my thesis

Background for the Digital Wellbeing baseline in my proposal: Google's own researchers, the year Digital Wellbeing launched (2018), recommended usage metrics, user-defined app and time limits, overridable lockouts and partial disconnection, which closely resemble the dashboard, app timers, Focus mode and Bedtime mode that shipped (an inference; the paper does not name the product). Its "speed bump" with an override is the same friction-with-control idea tested by kim2019lockntype, haliburton2024 and lu2024, and its finding that unintentional checks bring the most regret matches cho2021. The obligation-to-respond cycle explains why notification and availability features matter, and the short-term forced disconnection turning into "freedom" parallels rosenberg2022's realizers.
