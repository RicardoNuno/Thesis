# schraggeova2025: The effect of digital detox through digital minimalism using the MinimalistPhone app on the behavior of young users and their emotional experience

**Authors / venue / year:** Schraggeová & Bisaha / Computers in Human Behavior Reports 18, 100699 / 2025
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chbr.2025.100699 (open access)
**Relevance:** medium

## Summary (3 sentences)

Slovak pre-post study with a control group (N = 57 Android users, mostly students) testing two weeks of MinimalistPhone, a commercial text-only launcher that replaces app icons with an alphabetical list, hides chosen apps, filters notifications, and asks users to set an intended usage time before opening apps, with alerts when it runs out. Compared with the control group, users reported less habitual (automatic) phone use and their Android screen time fell more (about 58 vs 15 minutes per day), but self-reported "process" (entertainment) and social use and positive/negative affect did not change significantly. The authors interpret it as habit disruption by removing visual cues and adding steps, while warning that the groups already differed at baseline.

## Method

- Recruitment via Slovak university Facebook groups and flyers; 61 enrolled, 57 completed (experimental 29, control 28); aged 18+, Android only (the app has no iOS version); Nov 2023–Feb 2024, paused over Christmas.
- Assignment from a pre-generated random list in order of approach; researcher knew the next assignment; no blinding.
- Experimental group installed MinimalistPhone (free vouchers supplied by the company); control group got it after the posttest.
- Measures at pretest and after 14 days: habitual use (6 items adapted from Limayem et al.), process and social use (van Deursen et al.), PANAS framed on feelings about one's phone use, and screen time for 14 full days before and during, read from Android settings and sent as screenshots. Open-ended questions at posttest.
- Repeated-measures ANOVA (group × time), with Bonferroni-corrected within-group t-tests.

## Results / evidence

- Habitual use: significant group × time interaction (F = 22.6, partial η² = .29); experimental −17.9% vs control −1.2%.
- Screen time (14 days): experimental 4,168 → 3,362 min, control 3,135 → 2,925 min; interaction significant (F = 5.58, p = .022, partial η² = .09); −15.2% vs −6.0%. 72% of participants reduced screen time.
- Process use: no significant interaction (within-group drop of 8% in the experimental group). Social use: no change.
- Affect: no significant change; more variability in the experimental group, with negative affect falling slightly more.
- Open answers: 19 of 29 positive, 9 neutral, 1 negative; "The more steps I had to go through before mindless scrolling, the more time I had for self-control"; others dismissed the time-limit alert and kept scrolling.

## Strengths / limitations

- Strength: tests a real, widely available Android tool (a launcher, needing no special permissions beyond being the home app) with a control group and screen time from the OS rather than recall.
- Strength: specific mechanism (remove visual cues, add steps, set intention) tied to habit theory.
- Limitation: groups differed at baseline (the experimental group used the phone ~1 h/day more and scored higher on habitual use), so the main effects may partly be regression to the mean; the authors admit selection bias because the researcher knew the assignment order.
- Limitation: small sample, two weeks, no follow-up after removal; only total screen time (no pickups or per-app data).
- Limitation: the company supplied the vouchers; no conflict of interest discussion beyond that.
- Limitation: social-use scale reliability was low (ω = .63); explaining high screen time by "the COVID-19 lockdown period" does not fit 2023–24 data.

## How it relates to my thesis

A close real-world example of a lightweight Android intervention: a home-screen replacement that changes the interface instead of blocking, needs no Accessibility or usage-access permission for its core idea, and is evaluated against screen time from Digital Wellbeing, all directly relevant to my proposal's criteria. Its mechanism (remove icons as cues, add a step and an intention before opening) is a cue-removal and friction design comparable to haliburton2024's one sec and okeke2018's nudges, and matches oulasvirta2012's checking-habit account. Its weak points (baseline imbalance, no withdrawal phase, only total screen time) are the design issues an evaluation should avoid. Participants' comments that it only works for people who want to change match lee2026 and hiniker2016 on motivation.
