# rosenberg2022: The (other) two percent also matter: The construction of mobile phone refusers

**Authors / venue / year:** Rosenberg & Vogelman-Natan / Mobile Media & Communication 10(2), 216–234 / 2022 (online 2021)
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1177/20501579211033885
**Relevance:** medium

_Skimmed from the accepted pre-copyedited manuscript; quotes cite sections._

## Summary (3 sentences)

Interviews with 25 Israelis (2017–2018) who voluntarily own no mobile phone at all, neither smartphone nor dumbphone, in a country where 2% of people have none. Refusers fall into two types: ideologists, whose refusal comes from a critical worldview about phones and often technology generally, and realizers, whose "post-factum resistance" began with a forced break (phone broken or stolen, backpacking trip) that turned out to feel freeing. Their objections are to the medium itself (portability, constant availability, intrusion into the home) rather than to any content or app, and they pay social costs (work scheduling, dating, pressure from family) that they consider worth it.

## Method

- 25 participants (14 men, 11 women, aged 18–60), recruited by a social-media post and snowball sampling from 55 names; checked that refusal was voluntary (not fear of technology or money).
- Semi-structured interviews of 60–90 min in Hebrew, mostly face-to-face (five by landline), grounded-theory style; individual profiles, then thematic analysis; three participants reviewed the draft findings (member checking).

## Results / evidence

- Ideologists: refusal part of a broader critique ("cold and extroverted culture", "shallow social discourse"), not necessarily anti-technology; all participants used the internet.
- Realizers: a forced break first felt like "the sky was falling", then brought quiet, attention to family, and the discovery that "nothing happened"; described as a "dramatic turning point" or "enlightenment"; they see it as a personal step, not a recommendation for everyone, and many call themselves technology enthusiasts.
- No phone even in a drawer: ideologists on principle, realizers because of temptation ("The mobile-phone can drag you back in an instant ... total non-use is better than partial use").
- They dismiss partial or temporary limits (detoxes, controls) as ineffective, blaming manufacturers' incentive to make phones addictive.
- Non-ownership practices: alarm clocks, maps, cameras, phone cards; borrowing strangers' or family members' phones, which they do not count as "use". All spouses and older children own phones; several refusers bought phones for their children for safety.
- Motives: protecting the home space (strongest theme), presence, privacy, mental health ("It allows me to stay sane"), some radiation concerns.
- Medium, not content: many would like the apps, just not on a device carried everywhere ("the Internet can be invasive once it's on your mobile-phone").
- Costs: coordinating, smartphone-only work schedules, a date ending because of no phone; some admit their life situation (no partner or boss) makes refusal easier.

## Strengths / limitations

- Strength: rare study of total phone refusers, with a useful distinction between ideological and experience-triggered refusal.
- Limitation: small Israeli convenience sample recruited via social networks, interviewed in 2017–2018; no generalisation claimed.
- Limitation: self-reports only; no measures of well-being or behaviour.
- Limitation: minor error: it calls Generation X "digital natives" and Y "digital migrants", which reverses Prensky's terms.

## How it relates to my thesis

Its main relevance is the medium argument: refusers object to the phone as a device (always carried, always reachable, intruding at home), not to its content, and many would keep using the same apps elsewhere. That suggests the form factor itself drives overuse, so moving some functions to a more limited device, such as a smartwatch handling notifications, calls and messages, could reduce reliance on the phone and make it easier to put down or leave behind. The paper does not study smartwatches; the link is an inference, supported by rothschild2024's participant who swapped a work smartphone for a cellular Apple Watch because it "does not invite endless interaction" (see the smartwatch section of the reading list). The "realizers" add that a short, even forced, break from the phone can turn into lasting change once people feel how little they lose, which a watch-based substitute could make possible without full disconnection. Caveats: a watch is also a carried, always-on device, and the refusers rejected partial measures (even a phone kept in a drawer) as too tempting.
