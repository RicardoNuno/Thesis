# larsen2023: Excessive smartphone use and addiction: When harms start outweighing benefits

**Authors / venue / year:** Larsen, Wiers, Su & Cousijn / Addiction 118(4), 586–588 / 2023
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1111/add.16060 (free: https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/121247585/Addiction_2022_Larsen_Excessive_smartphone_use_and_addiction_When_harms_start_outweighing_benefits.pdf)
**Relevance:** medium

> Editorial (opinion piece, 2 pages). No data, no study: it raises questions and sets a research agenda, it does not answer them.

## Summary (3 sentences)

Argues that calling excessive smartphone use an "addiction" is premature: there are no diagnostic criteria, most users occasionally show "symptoms", and the phone has real benefits that substances do not. Proposes the smartphone is better seen as a vehicle that makes other internet behaviours (specific apps) easier to overdo, like the cigarette made tobacco use easier. Calls for research on the minority with serious recurring problems, on specific patterns of use, and on the balance between benefits and harms over time.

## Method

- None. Opinion based on cited literature (panova2018, kwon2013sassv, sohn2019, Vuorre/Orben/Przybylski).

## Results / evidence

Main arguments (not findings):

- "Addiction" label is premature; prevalence estimates of 12.5–26.6% with substance-use criteria suggest over-pathologising.
- Habitual use is not the same as excessive or problematic use; frequent checking can be habit without harm.
- Addiction is better seen as excessive goal-directed behaviour, not extreme habit.
- The phone is a "catalyser" for digital availability: specific apps and features may be more addictive than others, like internet addiction is really problems with specific functions.
- Cigarette analogy: an easy portable device made tobacco use disorder more common; the smartphone may do the same for specific internet apps.
- Unlike alcohol or tobacco, smartphone use has many positive sides (communication, social contact, entertainment).
- Frequency of use is normal and high for everyone, so measuring frequency or "any symptom" is not enough.

## Strengths / limitations

- Strength: authors are addiction researchers, so the caution about the "addiction" label carries weight.
- Strength: clear, short framing of the debate; complements panova2018.
- Limitation: no evidence of its own; the cigarette comparison is an analogy, not tested.
- Limitation: raises "what would make it an addiction?" but gives no criteria or answer; explicitly says the right studies have not been done.

## How it relates to my thesis

Good framing for the thesis: talk about "excessive" or "problematic" use, not "addiction"; look at specific apps and patterns rather than total time (fits orben2019); and weigh benefits, not only harms. Its call to focus on "what patterns, why, and for whom" and on dynamics over time is something logged Android data can address. The stated goal, "a more sensible and healthy way of using the smartphone", is close to the thesis's aim.
