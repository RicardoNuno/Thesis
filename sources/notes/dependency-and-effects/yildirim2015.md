# yildirim2015: Exploring the dimensions of nomophobia: Development and validation of a self-reported questionnaire

**Authors / venue / year:** Yildirim & Correia / Computers in Human Behavior 49, 130–137 / 2015
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2015.02.059
**Relevance:** medium (the NMP-Q is the standard nomophobia scale; almamun2025 and leonmejia2021 prevalence figures are mostly NMP-Q scores; ~1,100 citations on OpenAlex)

> Article is paywalled. Notes taken from Yildirim's 2014 Iowa State MSc thesis, which the article is based on (same study, same data): https://dr.lib.iastate.edu/etd/14005. Page numbers below are thesis pages.

## Summary (3 sentences)

Builds the 20-item Nomophobia Questionnaire (NMP-Q), measuring "the fear of not being able to use a smartphone" rather than addiction. Items come from interviews with 9 heavy-using students, which gave four dimensions: not being able to communicate, losing connectedness, not being able to access information, giving up convenience. A factor analysis on 301 US undergraduates reproduced the four dimensions with high reliability, but there is no cut-off and no confirmatory analysis.

## Method

- Exploratory sequential mixed methods: interviews first, then questionnaire.
- Phase 1: 9 undergraduates (4 men, 5 women, 19–24) at Iowa State, chosen because they scored above the mean on 8 TMD dependence items. 10–15 min semi-structured interviews, phenomenological analysis in NVivo.
- Item writing: 23 items from interviewee statements; 2 experts (one the author's advisor) removed 3 duplicates; 2 students checked wording. 7-point Likert, total 20–140.
- Pilot: 86 students (87% women), alpha only; factor structure did not make sense, blamed on sample size.
- Main study: 301 undergraduates (166 women, mean age 20), stratified by college, recruited in classes.
- Analysis: PCA with varimax rotation, Cronbach's alpha, correlation with the Mobile Phone Involvement Questionnaire (MPIQ, Walsh et al. 2010) for validity.

## Results / evidence

- Four factors, 69.6% of variance (Table 5.4). The first factor alone explains 49.9% before rotation.
- Factors and alpha (Table 5.6): not being able to communicate (6 items, .939); losing connectedness (5, .874); not being able to access information (4, .827); giving up convenience (5, .819). Overall alpha .945.
- Correlation with MPIQ r = .71.
- Highest-rated items: annoyed if unable to use the phone (5.27/7), annoyed if unable to look things up (5.19), urge to check after a while without it (5.09). Lowest: "losing connectedness" items like being nervous about being disconnected from one's online identity (2.80) (Table 5.2).
- Daily use and checking frequency were collected but not reported.

## Strengths / limitations

- Strength: items come from users' own words, not adapted from substance addiction criteria like the SAS (kwon2013sas).
- Strength: measures one specific thing (fear of being without the phone), not a broad "addiction" label.
- Limitation: 9 interviewees, all heavy users from one university, define the whole construct.
- Limitation: PCA, not true factor analysis, and no CFA; the author lists CFA as future work. The large first factor suggests the four "dimensions" may mostly be one general factor.
- Limitation: validity checked only against another self-report phone-involvement scale (r = .71); nothing on anxiety, wellbeing or actual use.
- Limitation: no cut-off. The 20–140 score bands later used for "mild / moderate / severe" prevalence are not from this paper; they come from Yildirim, Sumuer, Adnan & Yildirim (2016, Turkish college students), per leonmejia2021.
- Limitation: one US university, mostly 18–21, women over-represented (55% vs 43% in the population; the authors admit this).
- Limitation: the author says in the thesis he was a "nomophobe" himself and believes people bond with their phones (p. 31), which may have shaped the interviews.
- Limitation: calls nomophobia a possible DSM-5 "situational phobia" (p. 71) with no clinical evidence.

## How it relates to my thesis

Another scale, like the SAS / SAS-SV, but for anxiety when separated from the phone rather than overuse. If the thesis cites nomophobia prevalence, it is almost always this scale, so the same caution applies: a self-report score from US students with no clinical cut-off. The four dimensions are useful to describe *why* people stay attached (communication, connectedness, information, convenience), which matters for design.
