# chu2023: Dose-response analysis of smartphone usage and self-reported sleep quality: a systematic review and meta-analysis of observational studies

**Authors / venue / year:** Chu, Oh, Gwon, Hwang, Jeong, Kim, Kim & Kim / Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine 19(3), 621–630 / 2023
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.5664/jcsm.10392 (free: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9978438/)
**Relevance:** medium

## Summary (3 sentences)

Meta-analysis of 17 studies (36,485 people) on smartphone overuse and self-reported sleep quality. Overusers have about twice the odds of poor sleep (OR 2.28), and each extra hour of daily use raises the odds by about 4%. Certainty is very low: almost all studies are cross-sectional, on students, with many different definitions of "overuse" and "poor sleep".

## Method

- Embase, Medline, Cochrane, Google Scholar, to January 2022; English only. PROSPERO registered.
- 17 studies: 16 cross-sectional, 1 cohort (COSMOS, which measured call duration, not smartphone use). Mostly students; Asia and Middle East plus UK, Finland, Sweden.
- "Overuse" defined in many ways: more than 4, 5 or 15 h/day; SAS-SV above cut-off (kwon2013sassv); use within an hour of bedtime; other overuse scales. So it mixes screen time and PSU.
- Sleep: mostly PSQI (with different cut-offs), insomnia scales.
- Dose-response on 5 studies; Newcastle-Ottawa for bias; GRADE for certainty.

## Results / evidence

- Pooled OR 2.28 (1.81–2.89), heterogeneity 80% (Figure 2).
- Dose-response: OR 1.042 per hour of daily use, linear (Figure 3).
- Publication bias (Egger p = .031); after correction OR 1.75. Without low-quality studies OR 1.94.
- GRADE certainty: very low (Table 3).
- No separate result for screen time vs PSU or for bedtime use.

## Strengths / limitations

- Strength: dose-response analysis, not only "overusers vs others".
- Limitation: mixes screen time, PSU scores and bedtime use under one "overuse" label, so it cannot say which matters.
- Limitation: cross-sectional; poor sleep could also lead to more phone use.
- Limitation: self-reported use and sleep; mostly students.
- Limitation: sloppy reporting (two different sample sizes, the cohort study measures calls, sensitivity analysis refers to case-control studies that are not included).
- Limitation: the REM point is weak (see below).

## How it relates to my thesis

Supports "more phone use, worse sleep" with a dose-response, a useful motivation for an app that tracks night-time use. Mechanisms suggested (blue light and melatonin, arousal, notifications at night) point to concrete targets for an intervention: use near bedtime and notifications at night.

**On REM:** REM is only mentioned in the discussion, as a possible effect of the phone's radio waves (RF-EMF), citing old lab studies (Borbély 1999, Loughran 2005) that found less REM sleep. None of the 17 included studies measured REM, and the authors themselves say the radio-wave effect is small compared with blue light. Not strong enough to cite as evidence; at most as a "proposed mechanism".
