# james2023: Understanding the construction of 'behavior' in smartphone addiction: A scoping review

**Authors / venue / year:** James, Dixon, Dragomir, Thirlwell & Hitcham / Addictive Behaviors 137, 107503 / 2023
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.addbeh.2022.107503 (open access, CC BY)
**Relevance:** high

## Summary (3 sentences)

Scoping review of what "behaviour" actually means in smartphone addiction research, i.e. what people do on their phones. Half of the studies measure no smartphone behaviour at all; those that do mostly ask for self-reported overall volume (screen time, kinds of apps, ownership length) rather than how, when and in what context the phone is used. Only about 10% use logged data, the share is falling, and the authors recommend logging by default, e.g. with Screen Time / Digital Wellbeing.

## Method

- Scoping review, PROSPERO-registered. Searched Web of Knowledge and Scopus (Feb–May 2022) for smartphone addiction, dependence, problematic/excessive use, smartphone use disorder, nomophobia.
- Inclusion: focused on smartphone addiction (usually has an addiction scale), primary data, English.
- 5434 screened, 1305 included (1,164,262 participants).
- Behaviours coded into 8 categories; logistic / quasi-Poisson regression on publication year to test change over time.

## Results / evidence

- 652/1305 (49.89%) report no smartphone behaviour.
- Behaviour categories (studies): screen time 418, type of use 331, length of ownership 130, context of use 128, pickups 102, preferred type of use 62, expenditure 43, self-reported addiction 39.
- Only 10.38% (n = 68) of studies reporting behaviour used logged data; share fell from 40% (2013) to ~9–11% (2019–2022).
- Fewer studies report behaviour over time (OR = 1.108 per year for "no behaviour", p < .001); field becoming more "trait-like", relying on scales.
- Logged use relates more weakly to addiction scores than self-reported use (citing parry2021).
- Self-report measures are inconsistent (free text vs ordinal bands with different cut-offs); no psychometric work on their bias.
- Samples are narrow: mean age ~21, 74.68% school or university students; ~40% of studies from China, South Korea, Turkey; 15% use bespoke or modified scales.
- Recommendations: log behaviour by default; use self-report only where needed; do intensive event-level tracking to model learning and habit; capture different pathways of problematic use; sample beyond students.

## Strengths / limitations

- Strength: very large review (1305 studies), pre-registered, data and code on OSF.
- Strength: concrete, practical recommendations (use OS screen-time tools).
- Limitation: English only, likely misses non-English literature from the main producing countries.
- Limitation: their definition and categories of "behaviour" are one possible choice; "context of use" lumps different processes.
- Limitation: maps measurement practice, does not test whether logged behaviour predicts addiction.

## How it relates to my thesis

Direct motivation for the thesis: the field claims "addiction" while rarely measuring use, and mostly through self-reported total time. Logged Android data on which apps, when, and in what patterns is exactly what the authors ask for, and event-level logging is what they say is needed to move from symptoms to process (habit, loss of control). Pairs with orben2019 (total time is not enough), larsen2023 (specific apps and patterns), and hitcham2023 (logged vs self-report follow-up by the same group).
