# clayton2015: The Extended iSelf: The Impact of iPhone Separation on Cognition, Emotion, and Physiology

**Authors / venue / year:** Clayton, Leshner & Almond / Journal of Computer-Mediated Communication 20(2), 119–135 / 2015
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1111/jcc4.12109 (free at OUP)
**Relevance:** medium

## Summary (3 sentences)

Lab experiment with 40 student iPhone users doing two five-minute word-search puzzles, once with their phone on silent at hand and once after the researchers took it away (claiming "Bluetooth interference"), placed it in a nearby cubicle with the ringer secretly turned on, and called it during the task. Compared with having the phone, being separated from it while it rang raised heart rate and blood pressure, increased self-reported anxiety and unpleasantness, lowered the sense of the phone as part of the "extended self", and reduced puzzle performance (about 8.4 vs 6 words found). In post-hoc regressions, the anxiety participants reported predicted the drop in performance, while the urge to answer did not.

## Method

- Recruitment: 136 journalism students surveyed under a cover story ("media usage"); 117 iPhone users invited; 41 took part, 1 excluded for answering the phone → N = 40 (73% women, mean age 21, 88% White). Extra credit and a $50 gift-card draw for puzzle performance.
- Within-subjects, counterbalanced order (possession first or separation first, 20 per order).
- Separation: phone moved ~4 feet away to another cubicle, silent mode disabled, researchers called at minute 3 (six rings, ~20 s); possession: phone on silent on the desk.
- Measures: blood pressure and heart rate (wireless cuff, baseline plus one reading at minute 4 of each puzzle); STAI-6 state anxiety; SAM pleasantness/unpleasantness; Extended Self scale; words found. Repeated-measures ANCOVA controlling for self-reported iPhone use.

## Results / evidence

- Extended self: 4.30 with phone vs 3.63 when separated (1–7).
- Performance: order 1, 8.50 → 5.50 words; order 2, 6.50 (separated) → 8.35 (with phone).
- Physiology: heart rate and systolic/diastolic pressure higher during separation in both orders (e.g., order 2 heart rate 87.3 separated vs 74.4 with phone).
- State anxiety and unpleasantness higher, pleasantness lower during separation.
- Participants reported a moderate urge to answer (4.6/7) and anxiety about not answering (5.2/7); anxiety, not urge, predicted poorer performance.

## Strengths / limitations

- Strength: physiological measures alongside self-report; cover story kept participants unaware (none suspected the call).
- Limitation: the separation condition combines two things, being without the phone and hearing it ring unanswered, while the "possession" phone was silent; the effect may be the unanswerable call (an interruption) as much as separation, as the authors acknowledge.
- Limitation: small, homogeneous sample (40 US students, iPhone only); one physiological reading per task; very large effect sizes (e.g., η² = .82 for heart rate) from a tiny sample should be treated cautiously.
- Limitation: only two short puzzles; no long-term or real-world measure.

## How it relates to my thesis

Evidence for the dependency side of the problem: being cut off from a ringing phone produces measurable stress and worse performance, consistent with nomophobia and fear of missing out. Paired with ward2017 (the phone's mere presence lowers cognitive capacity), it shows a dilemma for interventions: keeping the phone near distracts, but abruptly separating people from it, especially when it signals for attention, causes anxiety. That supports gradual or user-controlled friction over forced lockouts (kim2019goalkeeper, lu2024), silencing notifications rather than only hiding the device (olson2022nudge, aranda2018), and explains why people forced to switch to minimal phones initially felt more dependent (lee2026). Its confound also points to the notification, not the device, as the trigger.
