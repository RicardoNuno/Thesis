# hitcham2023: The relationship between smartphone use and smartphone addiction: An examination of logged and self-reported behavior in a pre-registered, two-wave sample

**Authors / venue / year:** Hitcham, Jackson & James / Computers in Human Behavior 146, 107822 / 2023
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2023.107822 (open access, CC BY; preprint: https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/tmhx5)
**Relevance:** high

> Skimmed from the PsyArXiv preprint; page numbers below are preprint pages.

## Summary (3 sentences)

Pre-registered two-wave study (511 users, 391 retested after two weeks) comparing logged screen time (iOS Screen Time / Android Digital Wellbeing) and self-reported screen time as predictors of smartphone addiction (SABAS). Self-reported use predicts addiction more strongly than logged use, and about half of the link between logged use and addiction runs through perceived use; people who overestimate their use report more addiction. Pickups and notifications are not related to addiction, so the authors argue that addiction scales reflect perceived overuse, distress and impulsivity (negative urgency) more than actual behaviour.

## Method

- 511 smartphone users (Prolific + volunteers; mean age 27; 288 Android, 223 iOS; 30 countries); 391 at T2 two weeks later.
- Logged data: participants copied screen time, notifications and pickups (iOS) or 7 daily screen times and notifications (Android) from their phone's settings.
- Self-report: "how much time do you estimate that you spend on your phone each day?"
- Scales: SABAS (smartphone addiction), s-IAT (internet), BFAS (Facebook), PHQ-9, GAD-7, short UPPS-P (impulsivity).
- Pre-registered correlations and regressions; exploratory mediation, misestimation score, response surface analysis.

## Results / evidence

- Logged and self-reported use were well calibrated on average (~4.5 h/day) and correlated r = .62 (higher than the parry2021 meta-analysis, r = .38).
- Logged use and addiction: r = .35 (T1), .28 (T2), weaker than addiction's links with depression, anxiety and other "addictions".
- Perceived use mediated 52% (T1) and 56% (T2) of the logged use → addiction effect.
- Overestimating use correlated with higher addiction scores (r = -.10 / -.14); absolute error did not, so "addicted" people are not just worse estimators.
- High perceived + low logged use → more addiction; the reverse not.
- Pickups and notifications not associated with addiction or distress.
- Negative urgency (acting rashly when distressed) predicted addiction at both waves; depression was inconsistent.

## Strengths / limitations

- Strength: pre-registered, retest, real logged data, open data and code on OSF.
- Strength: same group as james2023; puts its recommendation into practice.
- Limitation: "logged" data is copied by hand from settings screens, only aggregates (no apps, no events).
- Limitation: online convenience sample, short two-week span.
- Limitation: only total time; the authors themselves say aggregate time cannot capture type of interaction (e.g. posting vs scrolling).

## How it relates to my thesis

Strong evidence that total screen time, even logged, explains little of "addiction", and that self-perception does a lot of the work. Supports measuring what the time is spent on (apps, patterns, context) rather than volume, which is exactly what the thesis's Android logging can do and what this study could not. Also shows that OS screen-time data is accessible and enabled by most users (84.5% at T1, 95.2% at T2). Pairs with james2023, parry2021 and orben2019.
