# orben2019: The association between adolescent well-being and digital technology use

**Authors / venue / year:** Orben & Przybylski / Nature Human Behaviour 3(2), 173–182 / 2019
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-018-0506-1 (free accepted manuscript: https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:5d844350-a359-47d3-b10c-4bfb93ce613b)
**Relevance:** high (main critical counterweight to "screens harm teens" claims)

> Skimmed from the accepted manuscript; no page numbers.

## Summary (3 sentences)

Re-analyses three large US and UK teen surveys (355,358 adolescents), running every defensible way of linking technology use to well-being instead of picking one analysis. The link is negative but tiny: at most 0.4% of the variation in well-being. Eating potatoes has about the same association, and wearing glasses a larger one, so the authors argue the weight given to screen time is not supported by this evidence.

## Method

- Datasets: Youth Risk and Behavior Survey (YRBS, US), Monitoring the Future (MTF, US), Millennium Cohort Study (MCS, UK).
- Specification Curve Analysis (SCA): lists all reasonable analysis choices (which technology measure, which well-being measure, controls or not) and runs all of them. 372 specifications for YRBS, 40,966 for MTF, 20,004 sampled from ~600 million for MCS.
- Bootstrapping (500 resamples) to test whether the overall pattern is above chance.
- Comparison specifications: the same analysis for other behaviours (bullying, marijuana, sleep, breakfast, potatoes, glasses, height, handedness…).
- Technology use: only self-reported answers to a few questions with fixed answer options, no logged data:
  - YRBS: how often they use electronic devices, and hours of TV on a weekday (7 options each).
  - MTF: one group was asked how often they use social media and read news online (5 options), and how much TV they watch (7 options). Another group estimated hours per week on internet, games, texting, calling, social media, video chat and school computer use (9 options).
  - MCS: hours per weekday of TV, games, internet at home and social networking (8 options), plus whether they own a computer.
- All cross-sectional, simple linear regressions.

## Results / evidence

- Median standardised β: YRBS −.035, MTF −.005 (not significant in most specifications), MCS −.032. Statistically above chance in all three.
- With good controls (MCS) the link nearly disappears: β −.068 without controls vs −.005 with controls.
- When parents rate the teen's well-being instead of the teen, the link disappears (β ≈ 0); with self-reports it is −.046. Suggests part of the link is the same person answering both questions.
- Social media use (MTF) β −.031; weekend TV slightly positive.
- Comparisons: being bullied and marijuana are 4.3x and 2.7x more negative (YRBS); sleep and breakfast are 1.7x to 44x more positive; eating potatoes 0.9x as negative (YRBS); wearing glasses 1.5x more negative (MCS).

## Strengths / limitations

- Strength: very large samples and transparent analysis; shows how much results depend on analyst choices, explaining conflicting findings in earlier work.
- Strength: the comparison with everyday behaviours makes the effect size easy to understand.
- Limitation: measures general "screen time" and technology use, not problematic smartphone use or dependence. Does not contradict sohn2019 / elhai2017, which look at PSU, not amount of use.
- Limitation: cross-sectional; cannot tell direction or cause (the authors say so).
- **Limitation: none of the measures is about smartphones.** Much of it is TV; the closest are texting, calling and social media. The result says little about smartphones specifically.
- **Limitation: use is self-reported, and the authors admit these measures are "noisy"**, so poor measurement may have made the effects look smaller than they are. Averages can also hide a minority who are badly affected.
- Limitation: only adolescents (around 12–18).

## How it relates to my thesis

Main counterweight: *how much* people use technology barely predicts well-being, so the thesis should not argue from screen time alone. Supports focusing on *how* the phone is used (problematic patterns, compulsive checking) rather than total time, in line with panova2018's caution. But it should not be cited as "smartphones don't matter": it barely measures smartphones, and its crude self-reported measures may make the effects look smaller than they are.
