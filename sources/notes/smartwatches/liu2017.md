# liu2017: Characterizing Smartwatch Usage in the Wild

**Authors / venue / year:** Liu, Chen, Qian, Guo, Lin, Wang & Chen / MobiSys '17, 385–398 / 2017

**Relevance:** low

## Summary (3 sentences)

Systems measurement study: 27 Indiana University students and staff wore an instrumented LG Watch Urbane (Android Wear 1.3) for 106 days, with a custom data collector logging device states, notifications, app execution, energy and network traffic. The watch was mostly dozing (dimmed face) and awake only 2% of the time, but woke about 72 times a day in short sessions, mostly "flick and look" glances (some accidental), push notifications from 200+ apps dominated by messaging and email, and a few longer interactions. Most energy went to dozing (56%) and the brief awake periods (27%), display and CPU dominate, and the authors suggest bundling delay-tolerant notifications and smarter push logic to save battery and avoid unnecessary interruptions.

## Method

- 27 participants chosen from 200+ applicants for diversity (18 students, 4 faculty, 5 staff; 7 women), each given the same watch, paired with their own Android phone; orientation sessions.
- On-watch collector (low overhead) logging screen/device states, notifications (Android Wear service and custom phone–watch channels), app activities and services, network flows; power models for two watches (< 6% error); trace-driven "what-if" energy analysis.
- Data were collected on the watch only; phones were not instrumented.

## Results / evidence

- States: dozing 50.6% of time; awake 2%; ~72 wake-ups per day, mostly short; triggers: flick-and-look (most common), notifications, longer user interactions (cards, voice, apps), unintended wake-ups from gesture misdetection.
- Notifications: from 200+ apps, mainly instant messaging and email; bursty (median inter-arrival 49 s); phone apps use crude push logic (e.g., WhatsApp pushes whenever its phone chat window is not in front; navigation keeps pushing to the watch even when the phone is mounted).
- Apps: only 2.8% of top free and 3.4% of top paid Play Store apps had a watch version; average 18 watch apps installed; execution dominated by background services rather than full-screen use.
- Energy: dozing 56%, awake 27%; display 30%, CPU 29%, network 3.4%; shortening the 5 s awake timeout saves 4–9% overall; bundling delay-tolerant notifications saves energy.
- Network: watch paired with the phone 84% of daytime; Bluetooth carries 91% of traffic.

## Strengths / limitations

- Strength: long deployment (106 days) and precise system-level logging, including energy models.
- Limitation: 27 people on one university campus, all with the same loaned watch and the same orientation.
- Limitation: phones were not logged, so the authors cannot say how the watch affects phone use (they say so explicitly).
- Limitation: Android Wear 1.3 era; focus is energy and networking, not wellbeing or behaviour.

## How it relates to my thesis

Mainly a technical reference if a watch companion is built: frequent short wake-ups and notification bursts dominate a watch's battery, so a companion that pushes or vibrates often costs battery, and bundling non-urgent notifications helps both energy and interruption. Its observation that phone apps push to the watch with crude rules supports filtering notifications before they reach the wrist (cecchinato2017, visuri2017). It says nothing about phone dependence despite the reading-list note.
