# chen2021: A comparative study of smartphone and smartwatch apps

**Authors / venue / year:** Chen, Chen, Liu, Chen & Li / SAC '21 (ACM Symposium on Applied Computing), 1484–1493 / 2021
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1145/3412841.3442023 (author copy: https://lilicoding.github.io/papers/chen2021comparative.pdf)
**Relevance:** low

## Summary (3 sentences)

Software-engineering study comparing 223 Android apps that have both a phone and a Wear OS version (Google Play's top 200 watch apps plus pairs from APKMirror), using static analysis of resources, permissions, components, code similarity (SimiDroid) and user-input event listeners. About 40% of resource files and roughly 30–40% of components/methods are reused between versions, watch apps declare less than half the permissions (7 vs 17 on average) and register about one-fifth of the click listeners, and watch apps are either standalone (145) or companion apps that rely on the phone app (78). Standalone watch apps are bigger, reuse more phone code, need more permissions and have more interactions than companion apps, which can hand tasks like accounts, sync and location to the phone.

## Method

- Dataset: top 200 Wear OS apps from Google Play and their phone versions, plus 95 pairs from APKMirror; 223 pairs after de-duplication; categories from Google Play.
- Standalone vs companion read from the manifest flag `com.google.android.wearable.standalone`.
- RQ1: resource reuse by file name and hash; permissions extracted with AAPT2.
- RQ2: component- and method-level similarity with SimiDroid (Jimple representation).
- RQ3: user-input event listeners counted after decompiling with JADX.

## Results / evidence

- Top watch categories differ from phone ones: Personalization (watch faces) 17% of top watch apps vs 2% of top phone apps; Health & Fitness and Sports also over-represented.
- Size: watch apps 5.7 MB vs phone 21.8 MB; standalone watch apps 6.8 MB vs companion 3.7 MB.
- Resources: ~40% of resource files reused (mainly PNG and XML).
- Permissions: phone 17 vs watch 7 on average (Communication 35 vs 15); INTERNET in 96% of phone vs 65% of watch apps; of 136 phone apps requesting location, 93 dropped it on the watch (presumably for battery); BODY_SENSORS appears mostly in health and watch-face apps; standalone 9 vs companion 5 permissions.
- Components: phone apps average 42 activities and 17 services; watch apps far fewer (standalone 15/7, companion 8/4).
- Code reuse: identical components 43% (standalone) vs 32% (companion); identical methods 36% vs 19%; non-identical code is mostly new rather than modified.
- Interactions: ~265 OnClickListeners per phone app vs ~51 per watch app (standalone 60, companion 33); scroll listeners slightly more common on the watch.

## Strengths / limitations

- Strength: concrete, reproducible description of how Wear OS apps relate to phone apps (dataset public).
- Limitation: static analysis only (affected by obfuscation, reflection, dynamic loading); counts declared permissions and listeners, not actual use.
- Limitation: mostly popular apps; no users, behaviour or wellbeing.
- Limitation: minor inconsistencies: summaries round reuse to "40% components and 30% methods" and call the standalone/companion difference "~6%" while tables show 43% vs 32% and 36% vs 19%; one figure caption (Fig. 4) describes permissions while the axis shows resource files.

## How it relates to my thesis

Its main relevance is that watch apps are designed with far fewer interaction points than their phone versions (about a fifth of the click listeners and a quarter of the screens), so there is simply less to do on the watch. This is a design-side explanation for the very short watch sessions measured behaviourally in pizza2016 and visuri2017 (median 2–5 s vs minutes on phones), supporting the idea that moving some tasks to a watch limits how far a check can turn into longer use; on its own, though, it counts code, not what users actually do. It is otherwise a technical reference if a Wear OS companion is built. It confirms the two architecture options (standalone vs companion, declared in the manifest), that a companion watch app can leave permissions and heavy work such as location to the phone (fewer permissions on the watch, consistent with a consent-friendly design), and that watch apps should be small with far fewer interactions than phone apps, as shahu2024's Watchful companion did. Can be dropped if no watch app is planned.
