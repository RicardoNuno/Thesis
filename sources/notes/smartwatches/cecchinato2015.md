# cecchinato2015: Smartwatches: the Good, the Bad and the Ugly?

**Authors / venue / year:** Cecchinato, Cox & Bird / CHI EA '15, 2133–2138 / 2015
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1145/2702613.2732837 (accepted version: https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/16616/)
**Relevance:** low

_Read from the City accepted version._

## Summary (3 sentences)

Late-breaking work reporting initial findings from 10 interviews with early-adopter smartwatch owners, an early stage of the study published fully as cecchinato2017. It proposes an updated definition of a smartwatch (wrist-worn, computational, short-range connected, notifications, sensors and data storage, integrated clock) and finds owners used watches variously for notifications, as an augmented watch, for health tracking or entertainment. "The good" was unobtrusive notifications that make people less dependent on the phone in social situations; "the bad" was unclear added value compared with a smartphone; "the ugly" was aesthetics, which depend strongly on personal taste.

## Method

- 10 semi-structured interviews; UK, owned a smartwatch ≥ 3 weeks (up to 2 years); all men, aged 26–63 (mean 34.3), mostly tech/UX jobs; Pebble (5), Moto 360 (2), LG G Watch (2), Samsung Gear S (1). Self-described innovators (7), early adopters (2), early majority (1).
- Ongoing thematic analysis.

## Results / evidence

- Main motive for buying: curiosity ("it's what's next"); one bought a Pebble for sleep monitoring.
- Uses varied: leaving the phone charging elsewhere and checking who messaged on the watch; running with maps, music and tracking split across phone and watch; a word-based watch face for someone who struggles to read the time.
- Main benefit: less phone dependence in company ("I'm less rude, I don't take my phone out of my pocket when I'm with people"); looking at a watch seen as more acceptable than looking at a phone.
- Seeing messages sooner but replying less ("I'm quicker at seeing [text messages], but I'm probably less likely to actually write a response").
- Limitations: owners themselves would not recommend them yet ("unless you really want to play with it, don't really bother"); not seen as truly "smart".
- 7 also owned traditional watches and 4 still wore them sometimes.

## Strengths / limitations

- Strength: early first-hand accounts from existing owners rather than lab users.
- Limitation: 10 men, tech-savvy early adopters, UK; preliminary analysis; no usage data.
- Limitation: superseded by cecchinato2017 (same study, 17 participants, plus autoethnography); 2014-era devices.

## How it relates to my thesis

Adds little beyond cecchinato2017, but has short, quotable evidence that owners see the watch as a way to keep the phone in the pocket during social situations, the core of the smartwatch-as-companion idea (shahu2024, bolle2015). Also a citable definition of a smartwatch.

## Quotes / figures worth citing (with page)

- Smartwatch definition: "a wrist-worn device with computational power, that can connect to other devices via short range wireless connectivity; provides alert notifications; collects personal data through a range of sensors and stores them; and has an integrated clock" (What is a Smartwatch?)
