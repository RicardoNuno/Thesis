# schirra2015: "It's kind of like an extra screen for my phone": Understanding Everyday Uses of Consumer Smart Watches

**Authors / venue / year:** Schirra & Bentley (Yahoo) / CHI EA '15, 2151–2156 / 2015
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1145/2702613.2732931
**Relevance:** low

## Summary (3 sentences)

Work-in-progress interview study with five smartwatch owners in the San Francisco Bay Area (late 2014) on why they bought a watch, how they use it, and what limits it. Viewing phone notifications was the most used feature; participants pushed all notifications to the watch without filtering, used first-party health/fitness and navigation apps, and rarely used third-party watch apps. Watches let them check notifications discreetly where looking at a phone would be rude, but they felt tethered to the phone ("an extra screen for my phone") and often switched back to it for basic tasks, so the authors recommend watch apps that work independently of the phone and cache data.

## Method

- 5 participants (2 women, 3 men, aged 18–51; only two in tech jobs), owned their watch ≥ 4 months: Samsung Galaxy Gear (2), Pebble (2), Moto 360 (1).
- One-hour semi-structured lab interviews (Oct–Dec 2014), showing apps on the device; grounded-theory affinity analysis of quotes.

## Results / evidence

- Purchase: price, phone compatibility and aesthetics; wanted a watch that could "pass as a watch"; both women found designs made for men.
- Notifications pushed to the watch "without using filtering options provided by the smart watch to further limit the amount of notifications they receive".
- Phone notifications often replaced dedicated watch apps (e.g., sports scores); health goal prompts motivated activity and wearing the watch.
- Public use: discreet checking ("it's way to make me less rude to people"), but talking to the wrist felt awkward; others worried about covert recording; owners used long sleeves to hide private notifications.
- Main limitation: dependence on the phone and Bluetooth range; basic tasks (email, photos, messaging) still needed the phone ("you might as well use your phone, right?").
- Recommendations: cache content so apps work away from the phone; fuller watch apps; care about the look of watch apps since they are visible.

## Strengths / limitations

- Strength: more diverse participants (age, gender, non-tech jobs) than other early studies.
- Limitation: five people, one interview each, early 2014 devices, US only; preliminary.
- Limitation: industry authors (Yahoo) aiming to design watch apps; no usage data.

## How it relates to my thesis

Mostly historical: an early account that notifications are the watch's main use. Its two points relevant to a watch companion are the opposite of what the thesis would want: owners mirrored all notifications without filtering (cecchinato2017 later found filtering after the novelty wore off), and the watch was useful but people "might as well use your phone" when it was nearby. That suggests a watch only reduces phone use if notifications are filtered and the watch can handle the action itself (bolle2015).
