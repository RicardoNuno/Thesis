# pizza2016: Smartwatch in vivo

**Authors / venue / year:** Pizza, Brown, McMillan & Lampinen / CHI '16, 5456–5469 / 2016
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1145/2858036.2858522
**Relevance:** medium

## Summary (3 sentences)

Twelve first-time smartwatch users wore an Apple Watch for a month, and on the last three days wore two cameras (one facing forward, one on the shoulder looking at the wrist), giving 168 hours of video from 34 days and 1,009 watch uses, plus interviews. Watch uses were very short (mean 6.7 s, median 2 s, versus a 38 s median for phone sessions in earlier work) and about 5.4 per hour; checking the time was 50% of uses but only 14% of watch time, notifications 17%, third-party apps almost nothing. The watch let people read notifications without taking out the phone or interrupting what they were doing (cooking, childcare, exercise, conversation), only about 3% of watch uses led to phone use, and no observed conversation was disrupted, though faster reading raised senders' expectations of quick replies.

## Method

- 12 participants (7 women, aged 23–36, median 30; five students), all iPhone users, no previous smartwatch or tracker; Apple Watch for one month.
- Final three days recorded with a "sensor bag": forward camera for context and a shoulder "stalk" camera on the wrist; participants could stop recording for privacy; 34 days (23 workdays, 11 weekend days), 168 h 21 min.
- Every watch interaction clipped (±10 s), coded for app, duration, people present; 110 clips analysed in depth with interaction/conversation analysis; 45–60 min interviews.

## Results / evidence

- 1,009 uses; 1.5–8.6 per hour (mean 5.4); duration mean 6.7 s (max 205 s, median 2 s).
- By frequency: time 50% (14.4% of time), notifications 17% (Messages 34% of 170 notifications, Activity 20%, third-party 20%), workout timer and notification centre next; Siri 2% (low trust in accuracy); third-party apps 1–2%.
- Triage: reading messages on the watch, replying later on the phone; only 13 replies on the watch; ~3% of watch uses followed by iPhone use.
- One participant: "I think I reduced my phone time by I would estimate around 60%" (self-estimate).
- Notifications during cooking, drilling, childcare and conversations caused little visible disruption; in 117 clips involving conversation, no case of talk being disrupted was found (68% of uses happened alone).
- The watch is "always there", hands-free to read; especially useful at home when the phone is elsewhere.
- Faster reading raised senders' expectations of quick replies; several participants were equivocal, feeling the flow of messages could intensify busyness.
- Usability issues: launching apps (getting lost in the app grid), small touch targets; swipes worked well.

## Strengths / limitations

- Strength: observed, not self-reported, watch use in natural settings; precise counts and durations; a rare look at use in social interaction.
- Limitation: 12 young, mostly urban Swedish participants, new to smartwatches, only one month (novelty) and three recorded days; Apple Watch only.
- Limitation: phone use was not measured, so the "reduced phone time" claims rely on interviews; ignored notifications are invisible on video.
- Limitation: small internal inconsistencies (time checks averaged 1.9 s in one place and 3.8 s in another; third-party apps 1% vs 2%).

## How it relates to my thesis

The best observational evidence for the watch-as-companion idea: watch interactions are a fraction of the length of phone sessions, and only ~3% lead on to the phone, so notifications triaged on the wrist rarely turn into the longer phone sessions that checking habits create (oulasvirta2012, ferreira2014). Complements cecchinato2017 (interviews) and bolle2015 (logging), but like them it never logs phone use, so "less phone time" is still unproven, a gap shahu2024 began to address. It also warns that a watch can increase reply pressure (aranda2018's obligation cycle) unless notifications are filtered.
