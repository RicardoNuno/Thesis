# cecchinato2017: Always On(line)? User Experience of Smartwatches and their Role within Multi-Device Ecologies

**Authors / venue / year:** Cecchinato, Cox & Bird / CHI '17, 3557–3568 / 2017
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1145/3025453.3025538 (author copy: https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1536348/)
**Relevance:** high

## Summary (3 sentences)

Qualitative study of existing smartwatch owners (17 interviews with early adopters who had owned a watch ~8 months on average, plus the first author's two-month autoethnography and comments from 60 friends and family) on how watches are used for communication alongside phones and laptops. The main benefit was glanceable notifications: a quick look decides whether something is worth interrupting the current activity for, without pulling out the phone, which participants said kept them from being drawn into other apps; limited reply options and the absence of read receipts on the watch let them delay replies and manage availability. After the novelty wore off most disabled some notifications on the watch, kept them on the phone, and created "microboundaries" (night mute, taking the watch off at home or at weekends, Bluetooth off), though the watch also risks becoming a "digital handcuff" and others came to expect faster replies.

## Method

- Autoethnography: first author's diary for two months from unboxing (Nov 2014 – Jan 2015), with photos and reactions of 60 people (A1–A60).
- 17 semi-structured interviews (~50 min, mostly Skype), winter 2014 – spring 2015, until saturation; recruited by flyers, ads, meet-ups, word of mouth. All men, aged 23–68 (median 31), technology-related jobs, 5.6 devices each on average; Pebble (8), Samsung Gear (3), Moto 360 (2), LG G Watch, Apple Watch, Basis Peak; owned 3 weeks – 25 months (median 5 months).
- Thematic analysis across both data sets; findings structured as receiving notifications → managing availability → replying.

## Results / evidence

- What: after an initial period, 9 of 17 turned off at least some watch notifications (7 disabled email entirely); they wanted control by person and topic, not just by app ("I want emails only from this specific person").
- How: glanceable triage was the main benefit ("seeing which ones are duds and which ones are actually ones I want to deal with"); glancing at a watch seen as less rude than a phone in meetings and conversations.
- Less phone engagement: "if I open the phone to check email then there are other things that you could very easily get into doing ... on a smartwatch you can't reply, so it's very much just reading and then dismissing a notification" (P5).
- When/where: night mute most common automated setting; others muted manually (dinner, holidays); 6 deliberately took the watch off to not be "online"; some turned off Bluetooth at home.
- Availability: reading on the watch does not trigger WhatsApp/Messenger read receipts, letting people delay replies; but some contacts expected faster replies once they knew the person had a watch.
- Replies: urgent → short reply on the watch; non-urgent → laptop if open (quick to type), phone rarely (cost of getting it out); one participant muted everything due to redundant notifications across devices and stopped using the watch for anything but time.
- Costs: privacy (others reading the wrist), distraction for bystanders, another device to charge.
- Strategies framed as microboundaries: social, temporal, digital (notifications only on some devices) and physical (not wearing the watch).
- Design suggestions: notification settings by contact (starred contacts, levels, working hours) rather than by app; location-based (geo-fenced) and activity-based notifications; modular wearables separating communication from fitness tracking.

## Strengths / limitations

- Strength: existing owners rather than novelty users; combines a long autoethnography with interviews; rich detail on cross-device behaviour.
- Limitation: 17 men, tech workers and self-described early adopters, mostly UK; data from 2014–15 with first-generation watches.
- Limitation: self-report only; the claim that watches "can reduce the time spent on other devices and consequently helping with issues of mobile addiction" was not measured (they call for logged cross-device data).

## How it relates to my thesis

Strongest qualitative support for the smartwatch-as-companion idea: the watch lets people triage notifications without unlocking the phone, which is exactly the trigger that leads into other apps (oulasvirta2012's checking habit), and limited reply ability acts as useful friction. It also shows the conditions: it only helps if notifications are filtered (otherwise it becomes another interruption source or is abandoned), and control should be by person and context, not just by app, matching the selective-control finding (parry2025, cho2021). The obligation-to-respond pressure seen in aranda2018 reappears here, with the watch able to both relieve and increase it. shahu2024 later tested this direction with logging, and pizza2016/mcmillan2017 and bolle2015 quantify watch-then-phone use.
