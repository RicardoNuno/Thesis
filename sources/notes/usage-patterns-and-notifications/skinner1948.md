# skinner1948: "Superstition" in the pigeon

**Authors / venue / year:** Skinner / Journal of Experimental Psychology 38(2), 168–172 / 1948
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1037/h0055873 (full text: https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Skinner/Pigeon/)
**Relevance:** medium (theory / framing)

## Summary (3 sentences)

Hungry pigeons were given food from a hopper at fixed intervals (e.g. every 15 s) regardless of what they were doing. Six of eight developed a distinct repeated behaviour (turning in circles, head-tossing, swinging the head, pecking at the floor), because whatever they happened to be doing when food arrived was strengthened and then more likely to be "rewarded" again by chance. Skinner calls this superstition: the bird acts as if its behaviour causes the food, and a few accidental pairings are enough to keep a behaviour going despite many unrewarded repetitions.

## Method

- 8 pigeons at 75% of free-feeding weight; a few minutes a day in a cage; hopper available for 5 s per reinforcement.
- Food on a clock (non-contingent, fixed-time), mainly every 15 s; for one bird later 1 min and 2 min.
- Responses counted by two observers; for one bird stepping was recorded mechanically (tambour) to get a cumulative record.
- Extinction (clock off) and reconditioning (clock back on) for that bird.

## Results / evidence

- 6/8 birds: clear, idiosyncratic responses, usually oriented to a part of the cage; none were present before the food schedule. 2/8 unclear.
- Repeated rapidly: typically 5–6 times per 15 s.
- Shorter intervals condition faster: 15 s very effective, 1 min much less so; once set up, the interval could be stretched to 2 min without weakening.
- Form drifts: one head movement slowly became a hopping step over many hours.
- Temporal discrimination: the bird waited 10–20 s after eating, then responded rapidly until food came, like a standard fixed-interval record.
- Extinction: over 10,000 responses before the hopping nearly stopped. Restarting the clock reconditioned it quickly (Fig. 1); after a second extinction a different response (walking) was picked up instead.
- Human analogies: card players' luck rituals; the bowler twisting after releasing the ball.

## Strengths / limitations

- Strength: simple, vivid demonstration that the timing of reward alone, not a real causal link, can build and maintain a repeated behaviour.
- Limitation: 8 birds, mostly qualitative description, no statistics; one detailed case.
- Limitation: later work (Staddon & Simmelhag 1971, Psychological Review) re-ran the procedure with closer observation and argued the behaviours are largely species-typical "interim" and "terminal" responses tied to the food schedule, not arbitrary superstitions. The interpretation is contested; cite it as an analogy, not as proof.
- Limitation: animal study; the link to smartphone checking is by analogy.

## How it relates to my thesis

Gives a mechanism for checking that keeps going when it usually finds nothing: if new content occasionally appears right after a check, the check is reinforced, and many empty checks do not quickly extinguish it (> 10,000 unrewarded responses here). Shorter intervals between rewards conditioned faster, which matches feeds and messages that update constantly. The temporal discrimination (waiting, then responding faster as the next reward is due) is comparable to the regular ~5 min pick-up interval in heitmayer2021 and the reward-based checking in oulasvirta2012. Also explains why a habit can be set off by cues rather than real need (89% self-initiated pick-ups, heitmayer2021). Caveat: framing only; the Staddon & Simmelhag reinterpretation should be acknowledged.
