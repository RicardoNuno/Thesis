# tran2019: Modeling the Engagement-Disengagement Cycle of Compulsive Phone Use

**Authors / venue / year:** Tran, Yang, Davis & Hiniker / CHI '19, Paper 312, 1–14 / 2019
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1145/3290605.3300542
**Relevance:** high

## Summary (3 sentences)

39 smartphone users aged 14–64 (high school, college, adults) did an interview, a think-aloud walk-through of the apps they check out of habit, and a sketching exercise to design a fix for their own use. Compulsive checking has a predictable shape: a few triggers start it (mostly filling empty or unpleasant moments, i.e. killing time) and a few end it (outside demands, a "30-minute ick" of self-awareness, recycled content). Participants judged use by whether it felt meaningful (an investment beyond the moment), deleted apps that hooked them without meaning, and almost all sketched lock-out mechanisms they then seemed reluctant to try.

## Method

- N = 39: 13 high school, 13 college, 13 post-graduation adults; 21 F, 18 M; mean age 21.9 (SD 8.9), 76% Asian; Seattle area; recruited by word of mouth, convenience and snowball sampling. Fall 2017–spring 2018.
- One in-person session (interview mean 22 min): (1) background interview on habitual use; (2) think-aloud demo of each app used out of habit ("what causes you to stop?", "does it feel meaningful?"); (3) pick one change, then sketch a design to support it.
- Grounded theory, open coding, protocol revised as themes emerged; $10 (adults) / $20 (teens) gift card.

## Results / evidence

- Triggers that start compulsive use (Table 1, p. 5):
  - Unoccupied moment: any downtime with no alternative stimulus ("killing time"; most common, nearly universal).
  - Tedious task: effortful activity, e.g. checking every 5–10 min during homework.
  - Social awkwardness: phone as cover when idle or embarrassed in public (mentioned more by teens).
  - Anticipation: expecting a social or informational reward (reply, email, likes).
  - Plus "nothing": an instinctive grab with no trigger the user is aware of.
- Triggers that end it (Table 2, p. 7):
  - Competing demands: another person, a task, driving, class (most common).
  - The 30-minute ick factor: sudden realisation of wasted time, often around 15–30 min ("What am I doing?").
  - Recycled content: seeing posts already seen, "when I refresh and there's nothing".
- Meaningful use = transcends the session (relationships, learning, self-development); most compulsive use described as meaningless.
- Downstream: nearly all had deleted at least one app that hooked them without enough meaning; some reinstalled because friends use it.
- Design: almost all sketched lock-outs (phone in jail, apps with padlocks, phone shuts down after 10 min, fast-draining battery), but were tentative about adopting them ("because I don't want to as well") → ambivalence: they want to be locked out and also keep using.
- Two cycles: short-term habitual checking and long-term reflection (dual-process self-regulation).
- Few age differences; the taxonomy applies to teens and adults.

## Strengths / limitations

- Strength: covers both ends of the cycle (start and stop), across ages 14–64; rich think-aloud data tied to specific apps.
- Strength: clear design agenda: move beyond lock-outs, design for meaning that transcends the session, design for "satisfying portion sizes" (meaning vs time).
- Limitation: small qualitative sample, one US tech city, mostly Asian and college-bound; all self-report, no logged usage.
- Limitation: taxonomies are descriptive; their predictive value is untested (authors' words).

## How it relates to my thesis

Explains the checking habit from the user's side, complementing oulasvirta2012 and heitmayer2021: most starts are about killing time, which are the low-reward checks people later regret (cho2021). The end triggers are directly useful for an intervention: "recycled content" matches cho2021's regret when following content is depleted, and the "30-minute ick" shows that a moment of awareness of time spent is what naturally ends a session. An intervention can create that moment earlier instead of blocking, e.g. a vibration that grows with session time so usage is felt in the hand. The lock-out paradox (everyone designs one, nobody wants to use it) is strong support for a non-restrictive, awareness-based design, consistent with cho2021's finding that blocking frustrates users.
