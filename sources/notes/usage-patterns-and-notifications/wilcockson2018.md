# wilcockson2018: Determining Typical Smartphone Usage: What Data Do We Need?

**Authors / venue / year:** Wilcockson, Ellis & Shaw / Cyberpsychology, Behavior, and Social Networking 21(6), 395–398 / 2018
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1089/cyber.2017.0652 (submitted version: https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/124444/1/CBSN_TW.pdf)
**Relevance:** medium–high (study design: how long to log)

## Summary (3 sentences)

Re-analysis of the andrews2015 dataset (27 Lincoln students/staff, 13 full days of screen on/off logs) to see how many days are needed to capture a person's typical use. Week 1 predicted week 2 strongly for hours (r = .81) and even more for checks (≤ 15 s uses, r = .96); checks reached r > .8 with the following week after ~2 days, hours after ~5 days, and weekends did not differ from weekdays. The MPPUS problem-use scale did not predict hours or checks, even with rank correlations.

## Method

- Same data as andrews2015: Funf in a Box app logging when the phone becomes active/inactive (screen, plus calls and music); 14 days, first partial day dropped → 13 days (week 1 = 6 days, week 2 = 7 days).
- N = 27 (17 F, mean age 22.5, range 18–33), University of Lincoln. Most installed on Thursday or Friday.
- Daily measures: total hours of use; number of checks (uses < 15 s).
- Correlate week 1 (single days, cumulative means of days 1..n, random day combinations) with week 2 totals; a priori target r > .8. Weekday vs weekend t-tests. MPPUS (27 items) vs behaviour, Pearson and Spearman.

## Results / evidence

- Week 1 → week 2: hours r = .81, checks r = .96.
- Weekday vs weekend: hours 4.95 vs 5.25 (n.s.); checks 41.0 vs 39.3 (n.s.).
- A single day vs week 2: mean r = .61 (hours, range .41–.74), .82 (checks, range .64–.89).
- Cumulative days: checks reach r > .8 after ~2 days; hours after ~5 days. Any 2 random days → r > .7 for checks.
- Checks and total hours do not correlate (all p > .1): separate measures.
- MPPUS predicted neither hours nor checks (Spearman, p > .07), for the whole period, any week, or any day.
- Lighter users were also less variable day to day.

## Strengths / limitations

- Strength: practical, testable guidance for study length; supports checks as a stable, cheap measure.
- Limitation: very small (27), young, one university; not new data (andrews2015). Here 27 analysed vs 23 in andrews2015, not explained.
- Limitation: correlations show that people keep their **rank** order across weeks, not that the absolute amounts are the same; group means across days are not reported.
- Limitation: "use" includes calls and music with the screen off (andrews2015 issue), so short "checks" may include non-user events (e.g. notification wake-ups).
- Limitation: the r > .8 target is arbitrary; no confidence intervals with n = 27.
- Limitation: claims checks are "more likely to be associated with problematic phone usage", but in its own data the MPPUS correlated with neither measure.
- Limitation: weekday effects (Mon vs Fri) not tested; first hours after install not analysed.

## How it relates to my thesis

Directly sets my logging duration: ≥ 5 full days for screen time and ≥ 2 for checks, so 7 days (one full week incl. weekend) is enough for a baseline; drop the partial install day. Supports checks (≤ 15 s; andrews2015, ferreira2014) as my main habit measure: more stable than hours and separate from them. Another result that the MPPUS does not track behaviour (andrews2015, ellis2019). The authors' reading that checking is automatic and therefore missed by self-report fits oulasvirta2012 and heitmayer2021. ellis2019 cites this paper for using 6 days of Screen Time data.
