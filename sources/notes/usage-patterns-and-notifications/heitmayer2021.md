# heitmayer2021: Why are smartphones disruptive? An empirical study of smartphone use in real-life contexts

**Authors / venue / year:** Heitmayer & Lahlou / Computers in Human Behavior 116, 106637 / 2021
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2020.106637 (accepted version: https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/107820/)
**Relevance:** high

## Summary (3 sentences)

Participants wore first-person cameras and then reviewed their own footage with a researcher (SEBE method): 37 users, 200+ hours, 1130 smartphone interactions. 89% of interactions were started by the user, not by a notification; phones were mostly on silent, yet users still felt disrupted, so the disruption comes from habitual self-interruption, not the device. Pick-ups were automatic and often unnoticed, users "fidgeted" with the phone for no reason, and got "caught in the loop" chaining apps; the gap between pick-ups (~5 min) stayed the same across places and company, only session length changed.

## Method

- SEBE (Subjective Evidence-Based Ethnography): eye-level "subcam" (7 g) worn ≥ 3 consecutive days, ≥ 5 h video each; then Replay-Interviews where participant and researcher watch the footage together.
- N = 37 (34 in quantitative analysis, 3 files corrupted), age 21–29, 43% female, mostly London, also France and Germany.
- Qualitative content analysis of interviews (6 themes; 3 reported: picking up the phone, notifications, habits & strategies).
- Quantitative coding of every interaction: duration, time since last interaction, location, context, notification (type), activity. App switches within a session coded as separate interactions; also re-run at session level (774 sessions). ANOVAs.

## Results / evidence

- 89% of interactions user-initiated; of the 11% triggered by notifications, 59% were visual only.
- Interaction duration: mean 64.4 s, median 23 s (25% ≤ 8 s). Interval between interactions: mean 290.5 s, median 137 s (25% ≤ 40 s). "One minute every five minutes" ≈ 10 min/hour.
- Most frequent activities: WhatsApp, lock-screen check, Instagram; calls, SMS, maps ~1% each.
- Lock-screen checks = every 6th interaction, mean 5.2 s; only 15.6% prompted by a notification.
- Longer when already on the phone than when coming from another activity (104 s vs 46 s) — the "loop".
- Facebook, Instagram, browser sessions significantly longer (feeds / endless scroll).
- Location and company change duration (longer at home and alone, shorter at work and in company) but **not** the interval between pick-ups.
- Notification-initiated sessions shorter (43 s) than self-initiated → notification use is purpose-driven, self-initiated use is distraction-seeking.
- 78% of sessions are a single interaction; same distribution whether notification- or user-initiated.
- Qualitative: pick-ups feel automatic/unconscious; boredom or frustration with the current task leads to drift; "fidgeting" (unlock, open/close apps, put down) by almost everyone, sometimes felt relaxing; all but two felt they spent too much time on the phone; participants surprised at their own frequency.
- Notifications: all called them disruptive and nearly always answered at once; double bind — silence the phone and the urge to check (fomo, social pressure) builds instead.
- Coping strategies: software (context-specific notification profiles, giving a friend the social media password) and hardware (face down, out of reach, off, under a pillow, earplugs, leave at home); "engage to not engage" at natural breakpoints.

## Strengths / limitations

- Strength: sees the off-screen context (what the user was doing before), which logs cannot; replay interviews reduce recall bias.
- Strength: directly shows self-report is wrong about one's own use.
- Limitation: small, young, mostly European student-age sample; participants chose when to wear the camera.
- Limitation: did not record notification settings; ANOVA assumptions violated (skewed data).
- Limitation: fidgeting looks like normal use in logs, so it is hard to scale up with logging — the authors flag this.

## How it relates to my thesis

Central for the thesis: if 89% of pick-ups are self-initiated, a tool that only manages notifications misses most of the problem; the target is the checking habit itself. Participants' own software and hardware strategies (silent mode, notification profiles, phone face down or out of reach) did not stop the checking: the phone was silent or out of range in most cases, yet 89% of pick-ups still happened, every ~5 min, and silencing made some check more (P29). Removing the external trigger alone does not work, which argues against notification-only or physical-barrier interventions. The fixed ~5-minute interval regardless of context supports measuring pick-up frequency/intervals from logs as a habit signal, separate from duration. Session length after a self-initiated pick-up vs after a notification is something Android logs can capture. Builds on oulasvirta2012 (checking habit, "gateway" → here "caught in the loop") and connects to ward2017 (mere presence), tran2019 (compulsive checking cycle) and the intervention section (friction / "ergonomic cost" as a design lever). The smoker comparison fits the reinforcement framing (ferster1957).
