# oulasvirta2012: Habits make smartphone use more pervasive

**Authors / venue / year:** Oulasvirta, Rattenbury, Ma & Raita / Personal and Ubiquitous Computing 16(1), 105–114 / 2012 (online 2011)
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1007/s00779-011-0412-2
**Relevance:** high

## Summary (3 sentences)

Three studies of early smartphones show that a large part of use is the *checking habit*: brief, repetitive inspection of dynamic content (home screen, email, Facebook, news), triggered by cues such as situations or boredom. Smartphones produce far more of these short sessions than laptops, are used for longer overall and more evenly through the day, and raising the reward value of quickly accessible content increases checking and also other app use ("gateway habits"). Users themselves do not see the habit as problematic; at worst it is "slightly annoying".

## Method

- Habit (Wood & Neal 2007): automatic behaviour triggered by situational cues. Habits ⊂ frequent behaviours; looked for sessions that are very fast, repeated in similar form, and tied to the same cue.
- Session = from device activation (idle/screensaver) to next idle/lock.
- **Study 1 (logging):** Android G1, US, N = 136 (median 52 days, 2009), sampled ~every 3 s; compared to laptops, N = 160 (~50 days, 2007), sampled every 1 s. Interviews with 20 + 15. Proxy for habit: **SIRB** sessions = short (< 30 s; thresholds 24 s phone / 29 s laptop), isolated (≥ 10 min since last session), reward-based (≥ 50% of time in reward apps).
- **Study 2 (A-B field experiment):** Nokia 6600, Finland, 2004–05, three groups (N = 5 + 4 + 6, 2–4 months). Intervention B adds awareness cues (location, Bluetooth presence, calendar) to the contact book.
- **Study 3 (diary):** 12 students given a Nokia N97, first 2 weeks, modified day reconstruction method + interviews; habit candidate = ≥ 10 uses in 2 weeks.

## Results / evidence

- Study 1: SIRB sessions pdpu: smartphone mean 3.39 (median 3.19, SD 1.88) vs laptop mean 0.39 (median 0.20, SD 0.52); KS p = 3.17e-44. Median sessions pdpu: 34.11 phone vs 7.39 laptop.
- Daily use: median ~160 min/day phone vs ~87 min/day laptop (p = 6.44e-8); phone use more spread across the day (entropy over 15-min blocks, p = 1.99e-20).
- Top G1 apps by time: home screen 21.77%, SMS/MMS 17.57%, browser 10.67%, calls 9.38%.
- More reward-app time ↔ slightly more SIRB sessions (r² = 0.031, p = .04) — weak.
- ~18% of G1 sessions brief and single-app.
- Study 2: of ~30k sessions, 35% "touching" (wake screen only, median 1 s) and 3.7% "scrolling" (contact book, median 7 s). Touching spread across waking hours, scrolling mostly afternoon/evening.
- Adding dynamic content: touching 5.4 → 12.1 pdpu, scrolling 0.1 → 0.9 pdpu, other app use 9.7 → 16.0 pdpu (all p < .001).
- Study 3: strongest habits are email, Facebook, feeds, news; 19 habit candidates in 9 people (1.67/person); 1.35 contexts explain ≥ 50% of a habit's occurrences. Habits in "empty" moments (lectures, commuting, home), alone, more on weekdays.
- Motives: entertainment, killing time / avoiding boredom, awareness. Only two used the word "addiction" (jokingly or "mild").
- Proposed reward types: informational, interactional, awareness.

## Strengths / limitations

- Strength: first strong evidence for checking habits, with logs, an intervention and qualitative data; the classic citation.
- Strength: A-B design gives causal-ish evidence that reward value drives checking.
- Limitation: very old devices (G1, Nokia 6600, N97), pre-notification-heavy, pre-iPhone-era apps; phone vs laptop data from different years and samplers.
- Limitation: tiny samples in Studies 2–3; SIRB is a proxy and thresholds are arbitrary; 3 s sampling misses very brief events.
- Limitation: authors frame habits as a design *opportunity* to increase use, not a harm.

## How it relates to my thesis

The origin of "checking" as the unit of interest: short, frequent, cue-triggered sessions, not total time. Gives a concrete operational definition (SIRB: short, isolated, reward-based sessions) that I can compute from Android logs, and shows the session-level approach james2023 asks for. The gap between heavy checking and users calling it "slightly annoying" connects to panova2018 (problematic use rather than addiction). Checking can also be framed as operant conditioning: new content arrives unpredictably, which is a variable-interval schedule (ferster1957) and produces frequent, persistent responding; Study 2 shows more reward → more checking. Once the habit forms it is cue-driven and can continue without reward, close to Skinner's "superstition" (skinner1948). Links forward to heitmayer2021, ferreira2014 (micro-usage), vanberkel2016 (session definitions) and tran2019 (compulsive checking).
