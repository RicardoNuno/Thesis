# ohme2021: Mobile data donations: Assessing self-report accuracy and sample biases with the iOS Screen Time function

**Authors / venue / year:** Ohme, Araujo, de Vreese & Piotrowski / Mobile Media & Communication 9(2), 293–313 / 2021 (online 2020)
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1177/2050157920959106 (open access, CC BY; PDF: https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/67943442/2050157920959106.pdf)
**Relevance:** medium

## Summary (3 sentences)

A two-wave Dutch panel of 404 iPhone users (age 18–68) estimated their daily screen time, pick-ups and notifications, then were asked to upload screenshots of iOS Screen Time a week later ("data donation"); only 47 (11.6%) did so successfully. Donators underestimated everything: 146 vs 257 min/day screen time, 55 vs 70 pick-ups (medians 25 vs 68.5), 42 vs 81 notifications; 70% under-reported time and 84% under-reported pick-ups, and self-report correlated with logs only at r ≈ .3–.4. Willingness to start collecting depended on technical skills; actually donating was linked to lower privacy concerns and lower privacy literacy, though these biases were small.

## Method

- Panel company sample of Dutch iPhone users, April 2019, n = 404 (55% F, M age 40, SD 13.7; 42% higher education). Paid €2.10 + €4.00.
- Wave 1: estimates of daily minutes of active use, checks (lock screen or unlock), push notifications; top 5 apps and websites; privacy concerns, mobile privacy literacy, self-rated phone savviness. Asked to enable Screen Time.
- Wave 2 (7 days later): step-by-step guide to screenshot and upload Screen Time (total time, categories, top apps/websites, pick-ups, notifications). Screenshots coded by hand by a research assistant.
- Errors: absolute error |self-report − log|, split into under- and over-reporting. t-tests and logistic regression for sample bias.

## Results / evidence

- Compliance: 404 → 307 agreed and enabled Screen Time (76%) → 122 finished wave 2 (40%) → 47 valid uploads (11.6% of total).
- Screen time: self 145.8 min (Mdn 120) vs log 256.8 (Mdn 237.5); absolute error 110 min; 70% under-reported (by 125 min), 30% over-reported (by 15 min).
- Pick-ups: self 54.9 (Mdn 25) vs log 69.6 (Mdn 68.5); absolute error 32; 84% under-reported (by 37).
- Notifications: self 41.5 (Mdn 11) vs log 81.4 (Mdn 44); absolute error 47.
- Self vs log correlations: screen time .37, pick-ups .32, notifications .36. Logged pick-ups and notifications r = .68.
- Top apps: Safari, WhatsApp, Facebook, Mail, Instagram; people named ~45% of their top-10 apps/websites correctly.
- Bias: starting to collect predicted only by phone savviness (OR 1.51); donators had lower mobile privacy concern (2.96 vs 3.28) and privacy literacy (1.92 vs 2.22) than non-donators; pseudo-R² .07 / .03.
- 3 of 47 donators had dating apps in their top 10: people still donate sensitive data.

## Strengths / limitations

- Strength: general-population sample (ages 18–68), not students; real OS logs rather than a research app.
- Strength: reports medians, which show the gap better than means (pick-ups 25 vs 68.5).
- Limitation: only 47 donators; accuracy results rest on them, and they differ from non-donators (lower privacy concern).
- Limitation: iOS only; Screen Time is visible to users (same issue as ellis2019); screenshots transcribed by hand; Screen Time itself not validated.
- Limitation: reporting errors: "the other 65" non-donators should be 75 (122 − 47); pick-up error described as "32 notifications" and notification under-reporting as "pick-ups"; says both funded by a UvA lab and "no financial support".
- Limitation: the bias analysis relies on a self-made, unvalidated "savviness" scale.

## How it relates to my thesis

Confirms with an adult, non-student sample what andrews2015 and ellis2019 found: people underestimate pick-ups (median guess 25 vs 68.5 real) and screen time, and self-report correlates only weakly with logs. Pick-up–notification correlation (.68) matches ellis2019 (.66). For my study design: data donation through Screen Time / Digital Wellbeing is cheap but loses most participants (12% completed) and selects for tech-savvy, less privacy-concerned people; a logging app avoids the manual step but has its own install bias. Also a counterweight to wilcockson2018's "short logging is enough": what matters too is who stays in the study.
