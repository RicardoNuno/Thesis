# stothart2015: The attentional cost of receiving a cell phone notification

**Authors / venue / year:** Stothart, Mitchum & Yehnert / Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance 41(4), 893–897 / 2015
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1037/xhp0000100 (paywalled; PubMed abstract: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26121498/)
**Relevance:** medium

> **Source note:** full text not accessible. This note is based on the abstract (PubMed) and the APS press summary (https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/motr/youre-distracted-even-when-you-ignore-that-text.html). Numbers below come from the press summary and are unverified against the paper. Cite only for the main claim.

## Summary (3 sentences)

Lab experiment: students did a sustained attention task on a computer while the researchers, without telling them, sent a call, a text message, or nothing to their own phones. Those who received a notification made more errors than controls, even though they did not pick up or answer the phone. The size of the drop was comparable to that reported in earlier studies where people actively used the phone (calls or texting); the authors attribute it to task-irrelevant thoughts (mind wandering) triggered by the notification.

## Method

- N = 166 college students, Florida State University (press summary).
- Random assignment to three groups: call, text message, no notification (control). Phone numbers collected in a demographic survey at the start, so notifications arrived on participants' own phones with personally relevant content.
- Task: Sustained Attention to Response Task (SART): press a key for every digit except 3.
- An experimenter recorded whether participants took out their phones; no instruction about phones was given.

## Results / evidence

- Notification groups made more SART errors than controls (abstract: "significantly disrupted performance").
- Press summary: receiving a notification gave "three times the probability" of an error; the comparison behind this figure is unclear.
- Effect comparable to active phone use (calls, texting) in earlier work (abstract).
- Group sizes, error rates, effect sizes: not available to me.

## Strengths / limitations

- Strength: controlled, randomised; real phones and personal notifications rather than a lab device.
- Strength: isolates the notification from actual phone use.
- Limitation: single lab session with students; short artificial task (SART), not real work or study.
- Limitation: "comparable to active use" is a comparison with other studies, not tested within this one (per abstract wording).
- Limitation: could not check stats, exclusions, or how phone-checkers were handled.

## How it relates to my thesis

The experimental evidence that a notification costs attention even if ignored: the disruption is not only the time spent on the phone but the interruption itself. Complements mehrotra2016 and pielot2014 (notifications are noticed even in silent mode and pull people in) and ward2017 (mere presence). Argues that an intervention should reduce or batch notifications, not only limit screen time. Counterpoint: heitmayer2021 shows most pick-ups are self-initiated, so notifications are only part of the problem.

## Quotes / figures worth citing

- "We found that cellular phone notifications alone significantly disrupted performance on an attention-demanding task, even when participants did not directly interact with a mobile device during the task." (Abstract)
- "The magnitude of observed distraction effects was comparable in magnitude to those seen when users actively used a mobile phone, either for voice calls or text messaging." (Abstract)
