# ward2017: Brain Drain: The Mere Presence of One's Own Smartphone Reduces Available Cognitive Capacity

**Authors / venue / year:** Ward, Duke, Gneezy & Bos / Journal of the Association for Consumer Research 2(2), 140–154 / 2017
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1086/691462
**Relevance:** medium

## Summary (3 sentences)

Two lab experiments with ~800 US undergraduates randomly placed participants' own phones on the desk, in their pocket/bag, or in another room while they did tests of working memory capacity and fluid intelligence. The closer and more salient the phone, the lower the scores, even when the phone was silent or switched off and participants said they were not thinking about it. The effect was strongest for the people most dependent on their phones; the authors argue that keeping attention away from the phone uses up limited attentional resources ("brain drain").

## Method

- Pilot (N ≈ 113): phone location (desk → pocket/bag → other room) predicts how top-of-mind the phone is; 67.5% usually keep the phone nearby and in sight.
- Exp. 1: N = 520 (of 548), 53% F, M age 21.1. Phone location: desk (face down) / pocket or bag / other room; phones silent, no vibration. Measures: Automated Operation Span (OSpan, working memory capacity) and 10 Raven's matrices (fluid intelligence); then self-reports on thinking about the phone and its perceived effect.
- Exp. 2: N = 275 (of 296), 3 (location; desk face up) × 2 (phone on silent / off). Measures: OSpan and Go/No-Go (sustained attention); exploratory 13-item reliance inventory → "smartphone dependence" (6 items, α = .89) and "emotional attachment" (5 items, α = .79).

## Results / evidence

- Exp. 1: location affected cognitive capacity (MANOVA p = .007, partial η² = .014); other room > desk (p = .002); pocket/bag in between and not different from either; linear trend desk → bag → other room. OSpan p = .02, Raven's p = .02.
- No difference in self-reported thinking about the phone (mode "not at all"); 76% said phone location did not affect them → people don't notice the cost.
- Exp. 2: location again affected OSpan (p = .03; other room vs desk M diff 4.67, p = .008); phone on vs off made no difference → not explained by notifications.
- No effect on sustained attention (Go/No-Go).
- Dependence moderated the effect (p = .04): no effect for low dependence, significant at mean and high dependence. Emotional attachment did not moderate.
- Face down, face up or switched off made no difference; only separation (another room) helped.

## Strengths / limitations

- Strength: large randomised experiments, uses the person's own phone, and rules out notifications by turning phones off.
- Strength: shows a cost people don't perceive, so self-reports would miss it (fits parry2021).
- Limitation: small effects (partial η² ≈ .014–.026); pocket/bag not significantly different from either condition.
- Limitation: lab tasks with US undergraduates; real-world relevance assumed.
- Limitation: dependence scale was exploratory and self-reported; moderation only just significant (p = .04).
- Limitation: cannot tell inhibition from hypervigilance (authors' words).
- Limitation: the effect is debated; parry2024brain is a critical meta-analysis of it, so read that before citing ward2017 strongly.

## How it relates to my thesis

Adds a cost of the phone that does not depend on checking it: the phone's presence alone takes attentional resources, more so for dependent users. Useful for the motivation, but with care because of the replication debate (parry2024brain). For intervention design it cuts both ways: an intervention that keeps the phone in sight (or makes it vibrate) may keep it salient, while "defined and protected periods of separation" are the one fix that worked. It also fits the smartwatch angle: if a watch handles notifications, the phone could stay further away (cecchinato2017, shahu2024).
