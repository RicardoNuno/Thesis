# duke2017: Smartphone addiction, daily interruptions and self-reported productivity

**Authors / venue / year:** Duke & Montag / Addictive Behaviors Reports 6, 90–95 / 2017
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.abrep.2017.07.002 (open access)
**Relevance:** low

_Skimmed from the Europe PMC full text (PMC5800562); quotes cite the section, not the page._

## Summary (3 sentences)

Online survey of 262 employed German adults linking smartphone addiction (SAS short version) to self-reported productivity, adapted from a work-impairment questionnaire (WPAI-GH, "health" replaced by "smartphone"). Higher addiction scores went with more work hours lost to the phone, a larger perceived hit to productivity at work and in daily activities, and fewer minutes worked without interruption. Everything is self-reported, and participants' own time estimates were inconsistent, so the authors call it a starting point for logged ("psychoinformatics") research.

## Method

- Recruited via a website promoted in German media and by the federal drug commissioner's office; Jan–Sep 2016; 605 completed → 262 analysed (employed, ≥ 14, smartphone owners; implausible answers and > 40 h/week workers removed). 168 F, mean age 32.
- SAS short version (kwon2013sassv), German translation, items reworded to first person, 0–5 scale (α = .87).
- Productivity: WPAI-GH items, plus copies with "smartphone use" instead of "health problems"; minutes worked without interruption; longest uninterrupted period.
- Spearman correlations, partial correlations controlling for ill health, post hoc mediation.

## Results / evidence

- SAS ↔ work hours lost to the smartphone (past 7 days): r_s = .37.
- SAS ↔ perceived impact of the phone on work productivity: r_s = .44 (.52 controlling for ill health).
- SAS ↔ minutes worked without interruption: r_s = −.37.
- SAS ↔ phone impact on daily non-work activities: r_s = .57 (strongest).
- SAS ↔ leisure hours on the phone r_s = .43; ↔ work hours on the phone r_s = .13.
- Hours lost partly mediated SAS → perceived productivity loss (Sobel z = 4.42).
- Average perceived productivity impact was low (1.88 on 0–10); people reported almost 2.5 h of uninterrupted work, which the authors doubt (checking is automatic, so it is under-reported).

## Strengths / limitations

- Strength: separates work from non-work impact; the larger effect is outside work.
- Strength: authors are frank about the limits of self-report and call for logging.
- Limitation: entirely self-report, cross-sectional, self-selected sample (people drawn to a site about smartphone use).
- Limitation: addiction scale and productivity measure both self-reported, so shared method bias inflates correlations.
- Limitation: unknown which apps were used; productivity is hard to operationalise.
- Limitation: mediation paths look mislabelled (the "direct" effect c' is reported smaller than the total effect c but described the other way round).

## How it relates to my thesis

Minor support for the motivation that frequent, automatic checking costs productivity and flow, especially outside work. Mainly useful as another example of what parry2021 warns about: an addiction scale correlated with self-reported outcomes, with participants unable to estimate their own use. Its cited figure (checking every 18 min, Markowetz 2015) and the note that checking is "automatic and unconscious" fit oulasvirta2012 and support logging checks rather than asking about them.
