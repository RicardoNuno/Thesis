# mark2018: Effects of Individual Differences in Blocking Workplace Distractions

## Summary (3 sentences)

31 information workers at a large US tech company worked one normal week, then one week with non-work sites blocked by Freedom (default list of 22 sites plus their own additions) and their phones put away. Overall, people rated their focus and productivity higher with blocking, but the benefit depended on the person: those with low self-control (less conscientious, less persevering, more drawn to social media) gained the most, while those with high self-control reported higher workload because, without online micro-breaks, they worked longer stretches without any break. Distractions also serve a purpose (short breaks, social contact), so blocking should be adapted to the individual rather than applied to everyone.

## Method

- N = 31 (of 32), mixed roles (admin, researcher, technologist, manager); $250 gift card.
- Week 1 baseline; week 2 Freedom blocking during work hours in locked mode, scheduled daily; phones in a drawer except for essential communication. Users chose sites to add to the default blocklist "so that they would control what sites to block"; work email not blocked.
- Measures: Cognitive Absorption scale (focused immersion, temporal dissociation, control, enjoyment, curiosity) at the end of each week; daily 6-item productivity self-rating; daily NASA-TLX workload; susceptibility to six distraction types; Big Five; UPPS impulsivity; 20–60 min interviews.
- "Self-control" groups from baseline CA Control score: low (n = 14) vs high (n = 17).

## Results / evidence

- Blocking week vs baseline: focused immersion up (22.9 → 25.7, p = .01); less temporal dissociation (more aware of time); lower enjoyment (p = .04); productivity up (26.5 → 30.3, p = .001).
- 17 said blocking had more benefits, 10 more costs, 4 neutral.
- Low self-control group: sense of control rose with blocking; workload unchanged. High self-control group: control unchanged, workload rose (interaction p = .02); 11 of the 16 who reported more stress were in this group, working longer without breaks.
- Self-control correlated with Conscientiousness (r = .47) and negatively with lack of perseverance (r = −.50).
- People most susceptible to social-media distraction gained the most focus (β = 2.70, p = .01); susceptibility to notifications did not predict it.
- Interviews: blocking cut "interruption residue" and "rabbit-holing"; people noticed their habitual reaching (typing facebook.com by reflex). Six disliked being controlled ("what is work or not is my choice").
- 20 would keep using blocking (esp. with changes), 5 maybe, 6 no.
- Users' design wishes: analytics and goals to learn self-control and depend less on the tool; block only at low-attention times (e.g. after lunch, when bored); suggest healthy physical breaks; allow short timed micro-breaks.

## Strengths / limitations

- Strength: real workplace field study; shows costs as well as benefits, and who gets which.
- Strength: concrete design recommendations from users who actually lived with blocking.
- Limitation: small sample, one high-tech company, one week of blocking; all outcomes self-reported.
- Limitation: no counterbalancing (baseline always first) and participants knew blocking was coming.
- Limitation: phones not blocked by software, only put away; social media not separated from other blocked sites.
- Limitation: "self-control" groups come from a single 3-item subscale split at baseline.

## How it relates to my thesis

Another argument for adapting the intervention to the person instead of one fixed block: the same restriction helps impulsive users and burdens self-controlled ones, who lose their micro-breaks. Complements kim2017pomodolock (user control makes blocking acceptable) and cho2021/tran2019 (blocking frustrates). Users' own suggestions point at awareness and learning (analytics, goals, asking "are you wasting time?") and at timing (intervene when attention is low), which is where an adaptive or AI-based approach could fit. It also shows that a short check can be a legitimate break, so an intervention should not treat every pickup as bad.
