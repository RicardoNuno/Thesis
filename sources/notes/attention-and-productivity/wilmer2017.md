# wilmer2017: Smartphones and Cognition: A Review of Research Exploring the Links between Mobile Technology Habits and Cognitive Functioning

**Authors / venue / year:** Wilmer, Sherman & Chein / Frontiers in Psychology 8, Article 605 / 2017
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.00605 (open access)
**Relevance:** medium

_Frontiers articles have no page numbers; quotes cite the section instead._

## Summary (3 sentences)

Narrative review of how everyday smartphone habits relate to three cognitive domains (attention, memory and knowledge, delay of gratification), plus executive function, academic performance, sleep and anxiety. Phones clearly disrupt ongoing tasks in the moment, but evidence for lasting effects is thin, mostly correlational and self-reported, and sometimes contradictory. The authors conclude that "not all smartphone use is created equal" and call for longitudinal work and for distinguishing specific types of use.

## Method

- Narrative (not systematic) review; Table 1 lists representative studies per domain.
- Explicitly excludes problematic use / addiction, radiation, and social-skills development.
- Notes the field's limits: almost no true experiments or longitudinal data, self-report usage measures of low reliability (andrews2015), questionnaires that date quickly, and measurement that can change the behaviour it measures.

## Results / evidence

- **Interruptions:** endogenous (own thoughts drift to the phone, often because the current task feels unrewarding) vs exogenous (notifications, cues). Within-phone interruptions can delay task completion by up to 400% (leiva2012); resumption errors rise steeply after 15 s, and phone interruptions often exceed that. A notification alone harms attention (stothart2015).
- **Media multitasking (MMI):** the Media Multitasking Index (Ophir et al. 2009) is a self-report questionnaire scoring how often people use two media at once. Heavy multitaskers filtered distractions worse and had higher task-switch costs; fMRI showed more right prefrontal activity, read as greater difficulty recruiting cognitive control resources (Moisala et al. 2016), and less grey matter in the anterior cingulate (Loh & Kanai 2015). Not phone-specific.
- **But not for all tasks:** several studies failed to replicate; heavy multitaskers were better at integrating visual and auditory input (Lui & Wong 2012); medium multitaskers may do best; action video games improve attention.
- **MMI flaws:** weights all 132 media pairings equally (music + messaging counts as much as gaming + reading), double-counts IM and SMS, doesn't separate game types. Still the most used measure.
- **Google effect / digital amnesia:** expecting to be able to look information up later reduces how well it is remembered; people remember where it is stored better than the information itself (Sparrow et al. 2011). Taking photos also weakened memory of objects (Henkel 2013); GPS navigation weakened spatial knowledge (Burnett & Lee 2005).
- **Immediate gratification:** heavier mobile users discount delayed rewards more (Wilmer & Chein 2016, mediated by impulsivity). In one experiment, non-users given a smartphone for 3 months became more immediacy-oriented (Hadar et al. 2015), suggesting heavy use can causally reduce the tendency to delay gratification. Media use promises cognitive needs but delivers emotional gratification (Wang & Tchernev 2012). "Likes" activate reward circuitry (Sherman et al. 2016).
- **Everyday functioning:** heavier use predicts lower GPA; effects partly indirect via sleep, mood and separation anxiety (Cheever 2014; clayton2015).

## Strengths / limitations

- Strength: broad, balanced map of the cognitive claims, separating acute from lasting effects and calling out weak designs (e.g. a confound in the Google-effect study, directionality in correlational work).
- Strength: useful pointers to primary studies (Ophir 2009, Sparrow 2011, Hadar 2015, Moisala 2016).
- Limitation: narrative, not systematic; no pooled effects.
- Limitation: much of the cited evidence is self-report and correlational, and from 2009–2016; some has since been questioned (brain drain: parry2024brain).
- Limitation: largely about media multitasking in general, not phone checking specifically.

## How it relates to my thesis

Gives the cognitive side of the motivation beyond time spent: interruptions and their resumption cost, weaker memory when relying on the phone, and a shift towards immediate rewards. The delay-of-gratification evidence links to cho2021's delay-discounting explanation of regretful use and to the reward view (skinner1948): checking offers a small, immediate reward over a larger, later one. The endogenous vs exogenous split maps onto self-initiated vs notification-triggered checks (heitmayer2021), which my logging should distinguish. Its main warning also applies to my thesis: claims should be about specific behaviours (checking), measured by logs, not general "smartphone use" (parry2021).
