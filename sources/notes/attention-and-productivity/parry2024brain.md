# parry2024brain: Does the Mere Presence of a Smartphone Impact Cognitive Performance? A Meta-Analysis of the "Brain Drain Effect"

**Authors / venue / year:** Parry / Media Psychology 27(5), 737–762 / 2024 (online 2023)
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1080/15213269.2023.2286647 (preprint: https://osf.io/tnyda)
**Relevance:** medium

_Skimmed from the PsyArXiv preprint (author's original, before peer review); numbers may differ slightly from the published version. Page numbers are preprint pages._

## Summary (3 sentences)

Pre-registered systematic review and meta-analyses of experiments testing whether the mere presence of a smartphone harms cognitive performance (56 effects, 27 studies, n = 7,093). Only working memory showed a significant pooled effect, and it is much smaller than in ward2017 (d = −0.20); sustained attention, inhibitory control, cognitive flexibility and fluid intelligence all came out null. Most studies are underpowered and use inconsistent manipulations, so the literature can neither confirm nor rule out the effect.

## Method

- Pre-registered on OSF; search covered 2014 (Thornton et al.) to October 2022; 25 publications, 27 studies; 32% of effects from student theses, 18% from pre-registered studies.
- Separate meta-analyses per cognitive function (robust variance estimation): working memory, sustained attention, inhibitory control, cognitive flexibility, fluid intelligence.
- Manipulations grouped as "proximal" (desk vs pocket/bag) or "distal" (desk vs other room / other side of room).
- Bias checks: funnel plots, Egger's test, influence diagnostics, equivalence tests (SESOI d = ±0.2), meta-analytic power analysis.

## Results / evidence

- Working memory (19 effects, 12 studies): d = −0.20 [−0.33, −0.07], p = .01; moderate heterogeneity (I² = 42%); no small-study bias.
- Sustained attention (13 effects): d = −0.14, n.s.; signs of small-study effects (Egger p = .004), driven mostly by Thornton et al. (2014).
- Inhibitory control (10): d = −0.05, n.s. Cognitive flexibility (7): d = 0.09, n.s. Fluid intelligence (5): d = −0.18, n.s.
- Equivalence tests could not rule out effects of ±0.2 either → data insensitive, not proof of no effect.
- Proximal vs distal comparisons did not differ.
- Median statistical power very low for effects of the size found; likely explains failed replications and inflated early effects.
- Ward2017's two studies have 2–4× the mean sample size of the others.

## Strengths / limitations

- Strength: pre-registered, separates cognitive functions, includes unpublished theses (less publication bias), open data and code.
- Strength: power analysis shows how weak the evidence base is, not just the pooled effect.
- Limitation: few studies for most outcomes (5–13 effects), so moderators and equivalence tests are underpowered.
- Limitation: one outcome metric chosen per task, guided by Thornton and Ward; other choices could change results.
- Limitation: did not test individual differences (e.g. smartphone dependence), which is where ward2017 found the effect.
- Limitation (preprint): calls the cognitive flexibility effect "negative" although d = +0.09.

## How it relates to my thesis

The counterweight to ward2017: the mere-presence effect survives only for working memory, and is much smaller than in the original studies. For the motivation I should cite brain drain cautiously ("a small effect on working memory"), or rather lean on effects of actually checking the phone (stothart2015, cho2021, tran2019), which is what my thesis targets anyway. Also another example of Parry's point in parry2021: small samples and weak measures give inflated effects, so my own evaluation needs an adequate sample size.
