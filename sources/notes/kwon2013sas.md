# kwon2013sas: Development and Validation of a Smartphone Addiction Scale (SAS)

**Authors / venue / year:** Kwon, Lee, Won, Park, Min, Hahn, Gu, Choi & Kim / PLOS ONE 8(2), e56936 / 2013
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0056936
**Relevance:** medium (foundational: the scale most PSU studies use, including olson2022; ~1,700 citations on OpenAlex)

## Summary (3 sentences)

Develops the Smartphone Addiction Scale (SAS), a self-report questionnaire, by adapting a Korean Internet-addiction scale to smartphones. Tested on 197 Korean adults, it shows six factors and very high internal consistency. The methods are weak, but the scale became the standard measure in the field.

## Method

- Started from the K-scale (40-item Korean Internet-addiction scale): "Internet" replaced with "smartphone", 2 items removed, 1 changed, 10 smartphone-specific items added, revised by six professionals. 48 items, 6-point Likert.
- 197 adults (of 214) from two companies and two universities in Seoul and Gyeonggi-do, Nov 2011 – Jan 2012. 133 women, 64 men; age 18–53 (mean 26).
- Validity checked against the K-scale and the Young Internet Addiction Test (also with "Internet" replaced by "smartphone"), a visual analogue scale of self-rated severity, and DSM-IV substance abuse/dependence items.
- Exploratory factor analysis (maximum likelihood, oblimin), loadings < .40 dropped.

## Results / evidence

- Six factors (61% of variance): daily-life disturbance, positive anticipation, withdrawal, cyberspace-oriented relationship, overuse, tolerance. The "disturbance of reality testing" subscale was dropped.
- 33 items loaded on the factors; 15 did not fit any factor.
- Cronbach's alpha .967 (subscales .83–.91).
- All subscales correlate with the K-scale (.44–.61), the Y-scale (.35–.50) and self-rated severity (.32–.57).
- Higher scores for students than professionals, and for high-school than master's/doctoral education.
- Score range given as 48–288 (higher = more addicted). No cut-off for "addicted".

## Strengths / limitations

- Strength: first smartphone-specific scale; items cover smartphone features (SNS, Wi-Fi, battery, charging).
- Limitation: built by swapping "Internet" for "smartphone" in an Internet-addiction scale, then validated against two other Internet-addiction scales with the same swap. The validity is partly circular: it shows the SAS agrees with scales built on the same assumptions, not that it measures addiction.
- Limitation: no clinical sample and no clinical interview, so no evidence it identifies real "addicts", despite calling itself a "self-diagnostic scale" and "diagnostic manual". No cut-off score.
- Limitation: small convenience sample (197), one country, 2:1 women to men, adults only. The authors admit the sample is not representative and "the theoretical base of this study was relatively weak" (p. 7).
- Limitation: inconsistent item count: only 33 items fit the factors, but the scale is presented as 48 items with a 48–288 range.
- Limitation: the "tolerance" factor is "trying to control use but failing", which is loss of control, not tolerance.
- Limitation: alpha of .967 is so high it suggests many redundant items.
- Limitation: the introduction's evidence for the problem is a media survey, a Live Science article and a BlackBerry user forum.
- Exploratory factor analysis only, no confirmatory analysis.

## How it relates to my thesis

Needed because most PSU prevalence figures (e.g. olson2022) are SAS or SAS-SV scores, so any limitation of the scale carries into them. Also explains panova2018: its list of "anecdotal" physical symptoms (lightheadedness, blurred vision, wrist and neck pain) is SAS items 3 and 4, and its salience example is item 21. Panova was critiquing this scale's items directly.
