# rogers2006: Moving on from Weiser's Vision of Calm Computing: Engaging UbiComp Experiences

**Authors / venue / year:** Yvonne Rogers / UbiComp 2006 (LNCS 4206), 404–421 / 2006
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1007/11853565_24 (author copy: https://oro.open.ac.uk/5253/)
**Relevance:** low

## Summary (3 sentences)

Position paper arguing that ubiquitous computing research inspired by Weiser's "calm computing" (context awareness, ambient intelligence, monitoring) has fallen far short of its vision, because modelling people's fluid, unpredictable behaviour and intentions is too hard and because automating decisions raises ethical problems (dependence, privacy, people being monitored without control). She proposes an alternative agenda of "engaging" rather than calming people: shifting "from proactive computing to proactive people", with bounded ensembles of technologies that help people learn, create, reflect and act, and that can be "exciting, stimulating and even provocative – causing us to reflect upon and think about our interactions with them". Three promising areas are playful/learning practices, scientific practices, and persuasive practices for self-monitoring and behaviour change.

## Method

- Critical review of a decade of UbiComp work (Weiser's "Sal" scenario, context-aware systems, IBM BlueEyes, assisted-living monitoring) and illustrative examples of engaging projects (PicoCrickets, Ambient Wood, Savannah, RoomQuake, seamful games, WaterBot, Pocket Pikachu). No empirical study.

## Results / evidence

- Context-aware and ambient-intelligence systems work only in constrained settings; when they guess wrong (e.g., a TV turning on because you stared into space) users are less forgiving than with people.
- Monitoring vulnerable people (care homes, the elderly) risks "panopticon" intrusion over which they have no control.
- Persuasive UbiComp: on-body and mobile sensing can show people dynamic information about their behaviour "at critically weak moments in a cajoling way"; "a constant but gentle 'nagging' mechanism may also be effective"; unlike care-home monitoring, the user is actively involved and owns the data, which eases privacy concerns.
- Calls for long-term studies showing that timely sensed feedback is what changes behaviour, noting that reminders and prompts have had mixed results because people relapse.

## Strengths / limitations

- Strength: influential, clear critique of the "do it for the user" automation agenda and a user-agency alternative.
- Limitation: opinion piece from 2006 (pre-smartphone era: PDAs, sensors); examples are mostly research prototypes for children and science.
- Limitation: the persuasive-technology section is brief and speculative.

## How it relates to my thesis

Background for the history paragraph: a counterpoint to weiser1991 showing that the field itself questioned "calm", invisible technology and argued for technology that keeps people in control and prompts reflection. That framing fits friction and reflection-based interventions (kim2019lockntype, haliburton2024, lu2024) over automatic blocking, and its persuasive-UbiComp section anticipates self-monitoring wellbeing tools while warning that people relapse. Its point that self-monitoring data owned by the user eases privacy concerns supports an on-device, consent-friendly design. Otherwise marginal.

## Quotes / figures worth citing (with page)

- "It argues for a significant shift from proactive computing to proactive people; where UbiComp technologies are designed not to do things for people but to engage them more actively in what they currently do." (p. 406)
- "we should also be designing them to be exciting, stimulating and even provocative – causing us to reflect upon and think about our interactions with them." (p. 412)
- "A constant but gentle 'nagging' mechanism may also be effective at persuading people to do something they might not have otherwise done or to not to do something they are tempted to do." (p. 416)
- "designing a device to be solely in the control of the users (and their social group) enables them to be the owners of the collected data. This circumvents the need to be centrally concerned with privacy issues" (p. 417)
