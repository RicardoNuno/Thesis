# haddon2011: Domestication Analysis, Objects of Study, and the Centrality of Technologies in Everyday Life

**Authors / venue / year:** Leslie Haddon / Canadian Journal of Communication 36(2), 311–323 / 2011
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.22230/cjc.2011v36n2a2322
**Relevance:** low

_Skimmed from the full text pasted into the session._

## Summary (3 sentences)

Review of domestication theory: a qualitative framework from UK media and consumption studies (Silverstone, Hirsch & Morley 1992; Silverstone & Haddon 1996) for how people fit information and communication technologies into their lives, what the technologies mean to them, and how they constrain or reject them. Haddon describes its strengths (rich context on why people use technologies as they do) and limits (time-consuming, focus on the home and household, little attention to culture or to the consequences of technology for well-being), and suggests narrower objects of study (e.g., the smartphone rather than "the mobile phone") now that the core technologies are no longer new. He also discusses how technologies become more or less central in people's lives, including lock-in when routines are reorganised around a device, "de-domestication" when functional alternatives appear, and non-users as critical consumers in digital-divide debates.

## Method

- Conceptual review drawing on the author's own studies and the domestication literature; no new empirical data.

## Results / evidence

- Framework processes: appropriation (acquiring), plus processes for placing a technology in the home's space and in daily time, and conversion (identity and display to others). This article labels the spatial process "incorporation" and the temporal one "objectification", the reverse of the usual definitions.
- Centrality grows with functionality, network effects (enough peers adopt), fashion, and lock-in: "we become locked into using some ICTs because we have reorganized parts of our life around them", e.g., spontaneous meet-ups arranged by phone; dependency studies and the phone as an "extension of the body".
- Centrality can also decline: "de-domestication" when an alternative appears, though in practice the old technology often changes role rather than disappearing (radio after TV, "remediation").
- Non-users are often critical consumers who can achieve their goals by other means, not simply people facing barriers.
- Limitation acknowledged by the author: domestication studies rarely ask about the consequences of technologies for quality of life.

## Strengths / limitations

- Strength: concise, authoritative overview by a founder of the approach.
- Limitation: pre-smartphone-era examples (home computers, early internet, mobile phones), no data on use or well-being; methodological focus.
- Limitation: stage definitions swapped relative to the standard framework (and to Haddon's own 2020 chapter).

## How it relates to my thesis

Only a theory label for something the empirical notes already show: people find it hard to cut down because daily life has been reorganised around the phone (aranda2018, rothschild2024, parry2025, rosenberg2022, baumer2013). The "de-domestication" idea, that a technology's role shrinks or changes when alternatives appear rather than disappearing, could frame the smartwatch-as-partial-substitute argument (rosenberg2022, shahu2024). Not needed otherwise.
