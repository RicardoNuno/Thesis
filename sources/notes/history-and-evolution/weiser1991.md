# weiser1991: The Computer for the 21st Century

**Authors / venue / year:** Mark Weiser (Xerox PARC) / Scientific American 265(3), 94–104 / 1991
**Link:** https://doi.org/10.1038/scientificamerican0991-94 (free text version: https://www.ics.uci.edu/~corps/phaseii/Weiser-Computer21stCentury-SciAm.pdf)
**Relevance:** high (founding text of ubiquitous computing)

## Summary (3 sentences)

Weiser argues that the most profound technologies are the ones that disappear into the background of everyday life, as writing and electric motors did. The personal computer has not: it remains "a world of its own" that demands attention. He proposes ubiquitous computing, many computers of different sizes spread through the environment, used without thinking, so that people can focus on their goals and on each other.

## Method

Vision / essay, based on prototypes built at Xerox PARC (tabs, pads, liveboards, active badges). No empirical evaluation.

## Results / evidence

- **The PC is still a world of its own:** over 50 million PCs sold, yet using one needs jargon unrelated to the task. Like the time "when scribes had to know as much about making ink or baking clay as they did about writing."
- **Disappearance is about psychology, not technology:** when people learn something well, they stop noticing it (reading a street sign). "Only when things disappear in this way are we freed to use them without thinking and so to focus beyond them on new goals."
- **Against the single box:** laptops, dynabooks and "knowledge navigators" are only a transitional step. A notebook computer "still focuses attention on a single box"; carrying a super-laptop "is like owning just one very important book."
- **Against virtual reality:** VR builds a world inside the computer and excludes "other people", "weather, grass, trees, walks, chance encounters". He proposes the opposite, "embodied virtuality": computers brought out into the physical world.
- **Electric motors analogy:** factories once had one big engine; cheap small motors spread into every machine (a car has 22+) and nobody notices them.
- **Tabs, pads and boards:** inch-scale (like Post-its, badges), foot-scale (like paper), yard-scale (like whiteboards). Goal: "hundreds of computers per room". Intimidating at first, like volts in the walls once were, but they will become invisible.
- **Pads are not personal:** "the pad that must be carried from place to place is a failure"; pads are scrap computers left lying around, like paper.
- **Social effect:** pushing computers into the background can "reverse the unhealthy centripetal forces" of the PC; ubiquitous computers "pose no barrier to personal interactions."
- **"The decline of the computer addict"**: like radio hobbyists disappeared once radios were everywhere.
- **Calm:** ubiquitous computing should "overcome the problem of information overload" and make using a computer "as refreshing as taking a walk in the woods."
- **Privacy** is named as the main risk: hundreds of sensing, linked computers per room.

On size: there is a theme of smaller, cheaper hardware (inch-scale tabs, the motor analogy), but shrinking is not the point. Weiser says ubiquitous computing "does not just mean computers that can be carried to the beach, jungle or airport". The point is many devices spread through the environment instead of one personal box that holds attention.

## Strengths / limitations

- Strength: clear, influential vision; defines the goal of technology that stays in the background.
- Limitation: an essay with no evaluation; the social effects (less isolation, decline of the computer addict, less overload) are predictions, not findings.
- Limitation: 1991 context: no smartphones, no mobile Internet, no social media, no attention-based business models. Predicts computing becomes ubiquitous over "the next twenty years", which happened, but through the smartphone: one personal box that demands attention, the opposite of what he described.

## How it relates to my thesis

Candidate opening for the thesis: it shows what was seen as the future of computing in 1991, which can then be contrasted with what actually happened.

Gives the ideal that PSU violates: technology should fade into the background, not "focus attention on a single box". The smartphone fulfilled "computing everywhere" but not "invisible": it became the single, attention-demanding, personal device Weiser called a transitional step. Its "centripetal forces" paragraph describes in 1991 what PSU studies now report (isolation, less face-to-face contact). It is also the starting point for distributing a phone's functions across other, less demanding devices, such as wearables.
